# CBT Workspace — Scientific and Clinical Review

**Review date:** 23 November 2026  
**Audience:** Adults using an educational, self-guided skills workspace  
**Purpose:** Explain the evidence base, the limits of emotion-specific claims, and the safety boundaries applied to the application.

## Executive summary

CBT is not one worksheet or one universal method. It is a family of structured psychological interventions that link thoughts, attention, emotion, physiology, and behaviour. The most appropriate techniques depend on the problem formulation and, when a mental disorder is present, on the disorder-specific treatment protocol.

The application therefore uses two different navigation layers:

1. **Common emotional experiences and thinking patterns** such as anger, stress, frustration, guilt, shame, jealousy, rumination, and low self-esteem. These pages offer carefully labelled CBT-informed skills. A page in this group is not evidence that the emotion is a disorder or that a dedicated treatment protocol has been validated for it.
2. **Condition-focused areas** such as social anxiety, panic, OCD, trauma reactions, and chronic insomnia. These pages explain established treatment components but also make clear when a validated protocol and a trained practitioner are needed.

The evidence map is deliberately conservative. Strong evidence for a diagnosis-specific treatment does not automatically establish the efficacy of every exercise on a website. Likewise, evidence that a general skill is useful in CBT does not mean that the skill is a complete treatment.

The site is an educational and practice resource. It does not diagnose, assess clinical risk, prescribe treatment, provide crisis intervention, or replace a qualified mental-health professional.

## 1. Scope and method of this review

### 1.1 What was reviewed

A targeted evidence scan was conducted for the application build, prioritising:

- Current or maintained clinical practice guidelines, particularly NICE, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and VA/DoD.
- Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised clinical trials.
- Component analyses where they help distinguish the contribution of specific CBT methods.
- Established CBT treatment manuals and professional training materials for descriptions of standard techniques.
- Primary trials and systematic reviews for more specialised processes such as rumination-focused CBT.

The search covered the following domains: adult CBT generally; transdiagnostic CBT; anger and aggression; elevated and occupational stress; generalised anxiety; panic; social anxiety; depression and behavioural activation; low self-esteem; repetitive negative thinking and rumination; perfectionism; OCD and exposure with response prevention; PTSD and trauma-focused CBT; and chronic insomnia/CBT-I.

### 1.2 Important limitation

This document is an **evidence map and targeted scientific audit, not a PRISMA systematic review**. It does not claim to have retrieved every paper, searched every database, assessed every included trial independently, or completed a formal risk-of-bias appraisal for every study. Search access was based on available online sources and published abstracts/guidelines. Some primary papers may require institutional access.

Consequently:

- The evidence labels are qualitative navigation aids, not formal GRADE ratings.
- They describe the strength of support for the named clinical approach or method in the stated context, not a universal ranking of exercises.
- A meta-analysis result is reported with its population, intervention, and comparator in mind. It must not be generalised to every user or every emotional situation.
- References should be revisited as guidelines and evidence evolve.

### 1.3 Evidence-label definitions used in the product

| Label | Meaning in this product |
|---|---|
| Strong | A recognised clinical guideline or converging high-quality evidence supports a defined intervention for the named condition. This does not mean the page itself is a complete treatment. |
| Moderate | Systematic reviews or controlled trials support the approach, but effects, populations, or protocols vary, or important qualifications remain. |
| Foundational / evidence-informed | The exercise adapts an established CBT procedure, but direct evidence for the specific emotion as a stand-alone target is limited or not established. |
| Limited / specialist context | Evidence for a universal self-guided intervention is weak, indirect, or highly context-dependent. The page is cautious and should direct users to assessment when symptoms or risk warrant it. |

The letter-style marker in the interface is a visual shorthand for these categories only. It is not a clinical grade, a predicted outcome, or an assessment of an individual.

## 2. What the wider CBT evidence supports

### 2.1 CBT across mental disorders

A 2025 unified series of meta-analyses examined 375 trials comparing CBT with control conditions across adult mental disorders. The authors reported significant reductions across the disorders studied, with effect sizes varying by diagnosis; results were large for several conditions including major depression, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, generalised anxiety disorder, OCD, and bulimia nervosa, and very large for PTSD and specific phobia. The authors also noted limitations in evidence quality. This broad review supports CBT as an important treatment family, but does not establish that one generic technique works equally well across conditions.

**Product implication:** use disorder-specific pages and avoid presenting one cognitive worksheet as a treatment for everything.

### 2.2 Transdiagnostic CBT

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis included 53 studies and 6,705 participants in trials of transdiagnostic CBT for emotional disorders. Compared with control conditions, the reported effects were g = 0.74 for depression and g = 0.77 for anxiety. The review found benefits at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up, but not at 24 months. The authors reported heterogeneity in design and methodological quality.

**Product implication:** recurring processes such as avoidance, threat appraisal, repetitive negative thinking, and emotion regulation can be addressed across diagnoses, while preserving disorder-specific treatment requirements.

### 2.3 Emotion regulation as a transdiagnostic process

A 2026 systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis included 619 studies, 41,590 clinical participants, and 36,787 controls. It found broad differences in self-reported emotion-regulation difficulties across mental disorders, including greater rumination and avoidance and lower use of acceptance, problem-solving, and reappraisal. These are group-level associations, not evidence that any single online exercise treats a disorder.

**Product implication:** the site can teach emotion awareness, problem-solving, and reappraisal as general skills, but should not imply that emotion regulation is the sole mechanism or a universal cure.

## 3. Audit by emotional domain and condition

### 3.1 Anger

**Evidence base.** Saini's 2009 meta-analysis included 96 studies and 139 treatment effects across nine categories of psychological intervention. The overall weighted standardised mean difference was 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.85), with substantial heterogeneity (I² approximately 66%). The included treatments were not all CBT. A 2018 review of meta-analyses reported that cognitive-behavioural treatments were widely used and that anger interventions showed at least moderate effectiveness in non-clinical and psychiatric populations, while aggression outcomes were less consistent.

A 2024 meta-analytic review of anger-management activities included 154 studies, 184 independent samples, and 10,189 participants. Activities that reduced arousal—such as breathing, meditation, and relaxation-oriented practices—reduced anger and aggression (g = −0.63; 95% CI −0.82 to −0.43). Activities that increased arousal, including cathartic venting, did not reliably reduce anger.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Functional analysis of trigger, appraisal, bodily activation, urge, behaviour, and consequence.
- Cognitive reappraisal of assumptions about intent, disrespect, and threat.
- Problem-solving and assertive communication.
- Relaxation or other low-arousal strategies to create time for a deliberate response.
- Relapse planning around predictable triggers.

**What the evidence does not justify:** anger is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis; the meta-analytic effect across psychological interventions is not a CBT-only estimate; anger reduction does not automatically imply reduced violence; and a brief online exercise is not an intervention for domestic violence, stalking, threats, or imminent aggression.

**Product decision:** mark anger as moderate and variable. Include a chain analysis, low-arousal pause, and thought review. Explicitly prioritise distance and urgent help where there is a risk of harm.

### 3.2 Stress

**Evidence base.** A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of internet-delivered CBT for adults with elevated stress or stress-related disorders included 14 comparisons and 1,831 participants. It reported a pooled post-treatment effect on self-rated stress of d = 0.78 (95% CI 0.66–0.90). The eligible studies were targeted at elevated stress; this result should not be presented as proof that an app can resolve objectively unsafe or excessive demands.

A Cochrane review on occupational stress among healthcare workers included 58 studies, 54 of them randomised, with 7,188 participants. It found low-quality evidence that CBT with or without relaxation did not clearly reduce stress at one month, while effects favoured intervention at one to six months and beyond six months in a small number of studies. CBT did not clearly outperform an alternative intervention in the limited head-to-head comparisons. The result is context-specific and quality-limited.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Stressor mapping and practical problem-solving.
- Cognitive work on catastrophic predictions and rigid performance rules.
- Behavioural planning, pacing, and graded task management.
- Relaxation and recovery as adjunctive strategies.
- Boundary-setting and help-seeking.

**Product decision:** mark stress as evidence-supported with important context qualifications. The page must not imply that distress caused by unsafe work, poverty, discrimination, caregiving load, or abuse is simply a thinking error.

### 3.3 Generalised anxiety and repetitive worry

**Evidence base.** A 2024 systematic review and network meta-analysis examined 65 randomised trials with 5,048 adults with generalised anxiety disorder. CBT, third-wave CBTs, and relaxation therapy outperformed treatment as usual on effectiveness outcomes. When high-risk-of-bias studies were removed, CBT and third-wave approaches remained superior; CBT alone was associated with long-term effectiveness in that analysis. Treatment acceptability did not differ clearly from usual care.

NICE CG113 recommends a stepped-care model. For more marked impairment or inadequate response to lower-intensity options, it recommends a structured high-intensity intervention such as CBT or applied relaxation, delivered by trained and competent practitioners. The guideline describes a typical CBT course of 12–15 weekly sessions, with adaptation according to need.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Self-monitoring of worry and functional impact.
- Cognitive restructuring of threat predictions.
- Worry postponement and discrimination between solvable problems and hypothetical worry.
- Problem-solving for actionable concerns.
- Exposure and practice tolerating uncertainty.
- Reducing avoidance and reassurance-seeking where these maintain the cycle.

**Product decision:** mark anxiety as strong for several defined anxiety disorders, but clearly state that the product is not a GAD treatment protocol. Present worry postponement as a skills exercise, not as a replacement for structured care.

### 3.4 Panic

**Evidence base.** A component network meta-analysis of 72 studies and 4,064 participants examined which elements of CBT for panic disorder were associated with better outcomes. Interoceptive exposure and face-to-face delivery were associated with better efficacy and acceptability. The analysis found lower efficacy associations for muscle relaxation and virtual-reality exposure in the examined packages. An APA practice guideline describes psychoeducation, self-monitoring, cognitive restructuring, exposure to feared cues, modification of anxiety-maintaining behaviours, and relapse prevention as components of panic-focused CBT.

**Product decision:** explain the panic cycle and provide a reflective record. Do not provide instructions for users to induce breathlessness, dizziness, tachycardia, or other sensations. Interoceptive exposure requires clinical assessment, screening for relevant medical risks, and a planned protocol. New, severe, or unusual physical symptoms must not be self-diagnosed as panic.

### 3.5 Fear and specific phobia

**Evidence base.** Exposure is a central behavioural component in CBT for phobias and several anxiety disorders. It is planned contact with feared cues to support new learning, not a dare, punishment, or requirement to tolerate unsafe conditions. Clinical guidelines for anxiety and social anxiety describe graded exposure, behavioural experiments, and reduction of avoidance or safety behaviours as protocol components.

**Product decision:** offer a ladder-planning worksheet only for low-risk, ordinary fears. The product must distinguish real danger from anxiety and must not encourage exposure to unsafe situations, trauma reminders, or medically risky sensations. Severe, complex, or disabling phobias warrant a trained practitioner.

### 3.6 Depression and low mood

**Evidence base.** NICE NG222, published in 2022 and last reviewed on 30 January 2026, includes structured CBT and behavioural activation among treatment options for adults with depression. The guideline describes CBT as goal-oriented and structured, focused on the interaction of thoughts, beliefs, feelings, and behaviour. It also describes behavioural activation as a structured intervention focused on the relationship between activity and mood, including avoidance and withdrawal. The recommended clinical formats involve trained practitioners and structured sessions.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Activity monitoring and activity scheduling.
- Graded task assignment.
- Activities linked to pleasure, mastery, connection, and values.
- Cognitive restructuring of automatic thoughts and core beliefs.
- Problem-solving and relapse-prevention planning.

**Product decision:** mark the treatment approaches as strongly supported for depression, but do not diagnose depression from a mood check-in. The app uses small activity-planning exercises and warns that persistent, severe, or risky symptoms need assessment.

### 3.7 Frustration

Frustration is a common emotional response to blocked goals, difficult tasks, and mismatched expectations. It is not a single psychiatric disorder with one validated CBT protocol.

**CBT-derived components:** ABC-style functional analysis, cognitive flexibility, problem-solving, graded task assignment, and behavioural experiments.

**Product decision:** label the content foundational/evidence-informed. The application must not claim that the three exercises are a validated frustration treatment programme.

### 3.8 Guilt

Guilt can be proportionate and motivate repair, or it can become persistent and excessive. It may occur in depression, OCD, trauma-related disorders, and other presentations. Responsibility appraisal and cognitive restructuring are used in relevant protocols, but there is no universal online guilt protocol suitable for all causes.

**CBT-derived components:** distinguish facts from hindsight, estimate responsibility proportionately, identify realistic repair, and reduce repetitive unproductive review.

**Product decision:** label as foundational. Trauma-related guilt, obsessional doubt, and severe depression require condition-specific assessment rather than repeated self-help disputation.

### 3.9 Shame

Shame can involve global negative self-evaluation, concealment, and avoidance. It is addressed in different therapies and may be part of trauma-focused treatment. The evidence does not justify presenting one generic worksheet as a validated stand-alone treatment for shame.

**CBT-derived components:** replace global labels with specific descriptions, test social predictions using carefully chosen behavioural experiments, and use fair evidence-based reappraisal.

**Product decision:** label as foundational/limited as a stand-alone target. Do not use reappraisal to minimise abuse, discrimination, exploitation, or another person's harmful behaviour. Trauma-related shame belongs in appropriate trauma-focused care.

### 3.10 Jealousy

Jealousy can reflect uncertainty, realistic relationship information, attachment concerns, perceived threat, or patterns of coercive control. It is not one diagnosis with a universal evidence-based CBT protocol. A generic cognitive exercise must not be used to invalidate credible evidence of betrayal or abuse.

**CBT-derived components:** distinguish observation from inference, identify reassurance/checking loops, and use respectful communication and personal boundaries.

**Product decision:** label as limited. The product explicitly prohibits using the exercises to justify tracking, surveillance, account access, threats, interrogation, isolation, or coercion. Delusional jealousy, stalking, violence, and coercive control require professional assessment and safety planning.

### 3.11 Rumination and repetitive negative thinking

**Evidence base.** A 2025 transdiagnostic meta-analysis included 55 studies and 4,970 participants. CBT interventions reduced repetitive negative thinking compared with control groups, with an overall post-treatment effect of g = −0.67. RNT-specific interventions showed a larger effect (g = −0.99) than less specific approaches (g = −0.56). A 2024 systematic review of rumination-focused CBT included 12 studies, 10 of them randomised trials, and described promising but preliminary evidence, while calling for more rigorous trials. Earlier randomised research tested rumination-focused CBT as an adjunct for residual depression.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Identify when thinking has stopped producing new information.
- Shift from abstract self-focused questions to specific, concrete processing.
- Separate actionable problems from hypothetical uncertainty and unchangeable past events.
- Use behavioural activation and planned attention shifts.
- Consider protocol-based rumination-focused CBT for persistent depressive rumination.

**Product decision:** mark as moderate, with a note that process-specific approaches are promising and protocol evidence is developing. Do not instruct users to suppress thoughts or assume that every repeated thought is pathological.

### 3.12 Low self-esteem

**Evidence base.** A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis examined CBT interventions based on the Fennell model of low self-esteem. The review reported promising changes in self-reported self-esteem, while noting uncertainty about whether these interventions differ from those targeting depression.

**CBT components with a rationale:** identify core beliefs and conditional rules, gather balanced evidence, test beliefs through behavioural experiments, and reduce overcompensation or avoidance.

**Product decision:** mark as moderate/promising rather than definitive. Avoid simplistic affirmations and avoid interpreting external mistreatment as merely a negative belief.

### 3.13 Social anxiety

**Evidence base.** NICE CG159 recommends individual CBT based on established models, including the Clark and Wells model and the Heimberg model. The Clark and Wells protocol includes work on self-focused attention, safety-seeking behaviours, video feedback, externally focused attention, behavioural experiments, core beliefs, and pre- and post-event processing. The Heimberg model includes cognitive restructuring and graduated exposure. These are structured treatment protocols, not simply exposure challenges.

**Product decision:** mark as strong for disorder-specific CBT. Explain components, but make clear that a full protocol should be delivered by a trained practitioner. Behavioural experiments should be planned and reviewed, not used to force social exposure.

### 3.14 OCD and exposure with response prevention

**Evidence base.** NICE CG31 recommends CBT that includes exposure and response prevention for adults with OCD, with treatment intensity and format adapted to impairment and preference. It notes exposure to obsessive thoughts and prevention of mental rituals or neutralising strategies when compulsions are not overt. It also emphasises appropriate practitioner training.

**Product decision:** mark as strong for ERP as a specialised treatment. The site explains the obsession–compulsion cycle and encourages specialist support. It does not provide an unsupervised ERP hierarchy or encourage repeated debate with intrusive thoughts. Reassurance can itself become a compulsion.

### 3.15 PTSD and trauma-related reactions

**Evidence base.** NICE NG116 recommends validated individual trauma-focused CBT interventions for adults with PTSD or clinically important symptoms, including cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy for PTSD, narrative exposure therapy, and prolonged exposure. It describes a structured course, commonly 8–12 sessions, delivered by trained practitioners with ongoing supervision. Treatment includes safety planning, managing arousal and flashbacks, processing trauma-related meanings, and reducing avoidance. Supported computerised trauma-focused CBT is limited to selected circumstances and includes exclusions for severe symptoms and risk of harm.

**Product decision:** mark trauma-focused CBT as strong for the indicated population but do not turn this site into a trauma-processing tool. No self-guided trauma-memory writing, imaginal exposure, or trauma narrative exercise is provided. The trauma page focuses on safety, education, support, and finding trained care.

### 3.16 Chronic insomnia and CBT-I

**Evidence base.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 clinical practice guideline gives a strong recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I in adults with chronic insomnia. Its recommendation was based on a substantial evidence base, including many randomised trials. The guideline gives conditional support to some single components such as stimulus control and sleep restriction, and advises against sleep hygiene as the sole treatment. The 2025 VA/DoD chronic insomnia guideline also recommends CBT-I.

**Product decision:** mark CBT-I as strong and condition-specific. Offer sleep observation and cognitive reflection, not a do-it-yourself sleep-restriction prescription. Sleep restriction can be inappropriate or risky for some conditions and safety-sensitive circumstances and should be discussed with a qualified clinician.

### 3.17 Perfectionism

**Evidence base.** A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis examined randomised controlled trials of CBT for perfectionism. This supports targeted CBT programmes for unhelpful perfectionism, although it does not mean that high standards or conscientiousness are pathological.

**CBT components with a rationale:** identify conditional rules, test feared consequences of mistakes, reduce unnecessary checking, and practise flexible standards through low-risk behavioural experiments.

**Product decision:** mark as moderate. All experiments must be limited to low-consequence tasks; no lowering standards in safety-critical, legal, medical, or other high-stakes work.

### 4.1 Cognitive restructuring — correct use

A sound thought record should:

1. Describe a specific situation.
2. Identify the automatic thought, image, or prediction.
3. Name the emotion and, when useful, rate its intensity.
4. Identify evidence supporting the thought.
5. Identify evidence that complicates it or has been overlooked.
6. Produce a balanced and credible alternative.
7. Choose a proportionate next action.

The aim is not to make every thought positive. A meta-analytic review of cognitive restructuring and psychotherapy outcome reported a positive process–outcome association, but the analysis included only four studies and 353 clients. The result is encouraging, but it is not proof that completing a digital worksheet alone causes clinical recovery.

### 4.2 Exposure — correct use

Exposure should be collaborative, graded, specific, and based on a formulation. The user should know the target prediction and what new learning is being sought. Exposure is not flooding, punishment, forced confrontation, or entering an objectively dangerous situation.

The application does not provide self-directed interoceptive exposure for panic, trauma-memory exposure, or full OCD ERP instructions.

### 4.3 Behavioural activation — correct use

Behavioural activation is not simply a list of pleasant activities. It involves monitoring the relationship between activity and mood, identifying avoidance, planning actions linked to meaning or reinforcement, grading tasks, and reviewing what happened. Motivation may not come first.

### 4.4 Problem-solving — correct use

Structured problem-solving is most appropriate when at least part of the problem can be influenced. The workflow is: define the problem, define a realistic goal, generate options, compare options, select a first step, and review. Repeatedly trying to solve an uncontrollable uncertainty can become rumination.

### 4.5 Relaxation and anger

Arousal-reduction strategies can be useful when high activation makes deliberate action difficult. The anger meta-analysis of arousal-related activities does not mean every relaxation method is a complete CBT treatment. It also cautions against equating venting or aggressive discharge with regulation.

## 5. Safety, escalation, and ethical boundaries

### 5.1 Scope of intended users

The product is written for adults. It is not a child or adolescent treatment tool and has not been clinically validated as a digital therapeutic.

### 5.2 Situations requiring professional or urgent support

The application should direct users towards qualified local support when symptoms are persistent, worsening, or interfere substantially with daily life. Urgent emergency support is appropriate when a person may act on thoughts of harming themselves or someone else, cannot remain safe, is in immediate danger, or is experiencing a psychiatric or medical emergency.

The product cannot identify a local crisis service without knowing the user's jurisdiction. It should therefore tell the user to contact local emergency services or a local crisis service and, where safe, a trusted person.

### 5.3 Specific boundaries

- No diagnosis or diagnostic score is produced.
- No medication recommendations are made.
- No trauma-memory processing is provided.
- No do-it-yourself panic-sensation induction is provided.
- No unsupervised full OCD/ERP protocol is provided.
- No sleep-restriction prescription is provided.
- No advice legitimises violence, coercion, surveillance, stalking, or privacy invasion.
- Exercises must not reframe genuine abuse, discrimination, unsafe work, or structural adversity as merely distorted thinking.
- Users should stop an exercise that increases distress substantially and seek appropriate support.

### 5.4 Why these boundaries matter

Clinical guidelines describe treatment protocols delivered by trained, competent, and supervised practitioners for several conditions. A self-guided website cannot reproduce the therapeutic relationship, individual formulation, risk assessment, medical screening, adjustment, or supervision built into those protocols.

## 6. Privacy and implementation audit

The current implementation is a static, browser-based application.

- No account, server-side database, analytics, or external AI service is required.
- Worksheet notes, check-ins, and plan completion are stored in browser local storage.
- Data remain on the current browser profile unless the user exports them.
- Users can export a JSON copy or clear local data.
- Local storage is not encrypted. Anyone with access to the same browser profile or device may be able to read saved entries.
- Private browsing, clearing site data, or switching browsers may remove access to saved notes.
- Users should avoid saving sensitive material on a shared or monitored device.
- External research references open in a new tab and are not used to transmit journal content.

The site imports no third-party application runtime. The stylesheet currently uses Google Fonts as a visual enhancement; system-font fallbacks preserve readability when the font service is unavailable. No personal journal content is sent to the font provider.

## 8. Maintenance requirements

Before adding a new emotion or technique:

1. Define whether the target is a common emotion, a transdiagnostic process, or a recognised clinical condition.
2. Identify the specific treatment protocol and population, rather than citing CBT in general.
3. Prefer current guidelines, systematic reviews, and randomised trials.
4. Record sample, comparator, outcome, effect estimate, and limitations when reporting quantitative findings.
5. Distinguish evidence for a whole treatment package from evidence for an individual component.
6. Add clear exclusions, practitioner requirements, and safety boundaries.
7. Update this review and the visible evidence note together.
8. Do not use generated AI text as a scientific source or invent references, treatment doses, effect sizes, or clinical recommendations.

**Bottom line:** the platform should make high-quality CBT skills easier to understand and practise while being honest about what has been validated, what remains an evidence-informed adaptation, and when self-guided work is not appropriate.

# Supplementary audit — expanded toolkit and new evidence (23 November 2026)

This section documents the second development audit. It evaluates the additional exercises added after the first release, records newly reviewed evidence, and states where the additional tools are adaptations rather than validated stand-alone treatments.

## 9. New evidence reviewed

### 9.1 Anger and emotion-regulation strategies (2025)

Pop et al. (2025) conducted a meta-analysis of 81 studies and 115 effect sizes examining associations between anger and emotion-regulation strategies. Anger was positively associated with avoidance, rumination, and suppression, and negatively associated with acceptance and cognitive reappraisal. Heterogeneity was large, and the included studies differed in anger type, clinical status, criminal status, culture, study quality, and sample composition.

**Interpretation:** this is an association meta-analysis, not a trial demonstrating that a particular worksheet or strategy causes anger reduction. It supports paying attention to rumination, avoidance, suppression, acceptance, and reappraisal in anger formulations, but it does not establish a universal recipe.

**Product changes:** the anger workspace now includes an anger-rumination interruption record and a structured assertive-boundary planner. The latter is a CBT-compatible communication skill, not an intervention for violence, coercive control, or imminent risk. The earlier 2024 meta-analysis of arousal-changing activities remains relevant: arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger/aggression on average (g = −0.63), whereas arousal-increasing activities did not show an overall benefit (g = −0.02). Venting should not be prescribed as an anger-management technique.

### 9.2 Behavioural activation for depression (2026)

Cuijpers et al. (2026) included 105 trials and 13,933 patients in a comprehensive meta-analysis. For adult outpatients, behavioural activation compared with control conditions produced an SMD of 0.67 (95% CI 0.54–0.80; 61 comparisons), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 71%) and a broad prediction interval. There was no significant difference between behavioural activation and other therapies in the included head-to-head comparisons. Self-guided behavioural activation also showed a smaller significant effect (SMD 0.36; 15 comparisons).

**Interpretation:** behavioural activation is an evidence-supported depression treatment, but effects vary and a self-guided activity worksheet is not equivalent to a complete behavioural activation protocol.

**Product changes:** the low-mood workspace now includes activity/mood/mastery monitoring and an early-warning/relapse-prevention plan. The latter is also consistent with NICE NG222 recommendations to identify warning signs, stressors, avoidance, and rumination and to make concrete contingency plans after improvement.

### 9.3 CBT-I components and delivery (2024)

Furukawa et al. (2024) analysed 241 trials involving 31,452 participants. The component network meta-analysis found associations with improved remission for cognitive restructuring (incremental OR 1.68), third-wave components (1.49), sleep restriction (1.49), and stimulus control (1.43). Sleep-hygiene education was not identified as an essential component in that analysis (iOR 1.01). The point estimate for relaxation was below one, but its confidence interval crossed one; it should not be interpreted as proof that relaxation is always harmful. The authors noted that possible component interactions could affect conclusions.

**Interpretation:** chronic insomnia treatment should not be reduced to sleep-hygiene tips. Multicomponent CBT-I is recommended by clinical guidelines; individual components need to be selected and sequenced within a programme suitable for the person.

**Product changes:** the sleep workspace now contains a stimulus-control review and sleep-related mental-arousal record. Both are educational/reflection tools. The site still does not calculate or prescribe sleep-restriction windows.

### 9.4 Health-anxiety psychological interventions (2026)

Lai et al. (2026), published online on 22 May 2026 in the *British Journal of Psychiatry*, conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of 35 trials involving 3,263 adults with substantial health anxiety. CBT, exposure therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, metacognitive therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and behavioural stress management showed significant effects compared with a waiting-list control. Component analysis linked exposure and response prevention, cognitive restructuring, and mindfulness with improved outcomes. The authors concluded that CBT and third-wave CBT are reasonable first-line choices for health anxiety.

**Interpretation:** the network comparison is not a licence to dismiss physical symptoms. The component analysis identifies associations within treatment packages; it does not prove that a self-guided exercise is sufficient or that each component is independently effective for every patient. Medical assessment and appropriate follow-up remain essential.

**Product changes:** a dedicated Health Anxiety workspace has been added. Its exercises map the anxiety cycle, distinguish medically advised monitoring from non-essential repeated checking, and support balanced appraisal after appropriate assessment. The page explicitly prohibits using the exercises to ignore new or worsening symptoms, cancel recommended tests, stop treatment, or delay urgent medical care.

## 10. Audit of the 34 additional tools

The added methods below expand the range of CBT skills represented in the platform. A technique can be a recognised CBT procedure without being an independently validated stand-alone treatment for the emotion to which it is applied.

### 10.1 Anger

**Interrupt anger rumination.** This tool targets repeated mental replay after an anger-provoking event. Its structure asks whether replay is generating new information, a repair action, or merely renewed activation. The rationale is supported by the 2025 anger/emotion-regulation association meta-analysis and by the transdiagnostic RNT meta-analysis. It should not be used to suppress a legitimate grievance or avoid a necessary conversation.

**Prepare an assertive boundary statement.** This converts blame or an aggressive urge into a behaviour-specific description, effect, request, and personal boundary. It is a CBT-compatible communication and problem-solving skill. It must only be used where direct communication is safe; leaving, distancing, or seeking help takes priority where intimidation or violence is possible.

### 10.2 Stress

**Sort demands by urgency and influence.** A demand-prioritisation record helps distinguish genuine deadlines and consequences from a subjective sense that every task is equally urgent. It is a structured problem-solving adaptation, not a validated stress treatment by itself. It must not imply that unsafe workloads or structural adversity can be fixed by individual organisation.

**Build an early-warning and response plan.** This transfers a recognised maintenance-planning principle into stress self-management: identify early signs, likely contexts, practical adjustments, support requests, and escalation steps. It is not a validated stress-relapse scale. Occupational support is appropriate when the source of stress is unsafe or unreasonable work.

### 10.3 Anxiety

**Audit reassurance and checking.** The exercise examines the short-term relief and longer-term persistence associated with repeated checking. This can be relevant to anxiety formulations, but checking may be a compulsion in OCD. Therefore, the tool explicitly directs users with severe or compulsive symptoms to an OCD-informed clinician and does not provide an ERP hierarchy.

**Test a coping prediction.** Cognitive work often addresses both the probability of a feared outcome and beliefs about its consequences or manageability. This exercise asks what coping resources and realistic choices would remain if the feared event occurred. It is not an assurance that the event will not happen.

### 10.4 Fear

**Record what exposure teaches.** Exposure learning should be assessed against a specific prediction and observable outcome, not simply by whether anxiety falls during the exercise. The record captures the prediction, step, observed outcome, and learning. It is restricted to safe and appropriate approach work; trauma processing and interoceptive panic exposure remain outside the self-guided tool.

**Map the cost of avoidance.** Functional analysis makes the immediate relief and longer-term costs of avoidance visible. The tool distinguishes fear-driven avoidance from reasonable precautions. It never instructs a person to approach a genuine hazard.

### 10.5 Low mood

**Track activity, mood, and mastery.** Activity monitoring is a core behavioural-activation procedure. Recording pleasure and mastery separately can help identify reinforcing activities and patterns of withdrawal. The 2026 behavioural-activation meta-analysis supports behavioural activation as a treatment for adult depression, but the site worksheet is an entry-level practice aid, not the whole treatment.

**Create a low-mood early-warning plan.** This helps identify personal warning signs and actions to restart when symptoms recur. NICE NG222 explicitly describes relapse-prevention skills such as reviewing what helped, identifying triggers and early signs, and making contingency plans. If symptoms are persistent, severe, or associated with self-harm risk, assessment and care take priority over this worksheet.

### 10.6 Frustration

**Adjust the goal without abandoning it.** This separates a valued outcome from one inflexible method, deadline, or route. It uses cognitive flexibility and practical problem-solving. It is evidence-informed rather than a validated frustration-specific treatment.

**Pause before acting on the urge.** This maps the need under an impulsive response—such as rest, information, help, or a revised plan—and asks for a proportionate next step. It is a CBT-informed response-planning skill, not a treatment for aggression or impulse-control disorders.

### 10.7 Guilt

**Draw a responsibility map.** Responsibility reattribution can help when a person takes total responsibility for an outcome involving several actors or circumstances. The worksheet prompts the user to consider information available at the time and to avoid hindsight bias. It is not suitable for self-directed checking when responsibility fears are part of OCD.

**Separate repair from self-punishment.** The tool distinguishes a specific, proportionate action from repetitive self-criticism after reasonable repair. It must not minimise real harm, legal responsibilities, or obligations to make amends.

### 10.8 Shame

**Replace the all-or-nothing self-judgement.** Continuum methods are used in cognitive therapy to move from global labels to more specific, evidence-based evaluations. Their use here is an adaptation; the site does not claim a validated shame-specific protocol.

**Examine the cost of hiding.** This explores whether concealment is a legitimate privacy or safety choice or whether shame-driven withdrawal is limiting support and valued activity. Disclosure remains optional. Users are never instructed to reveal sensitive details or disclose to unsafe people.

### 10.9 Jealousy

**Plan a response to the checking urge.** Functional analysis helps distinguish necessary information-gathering from repeated reassurance or checking aimed at certainty. The exercise prohibits accessing private accounts, devices, messages, or location data without consent.

**Clarify trust, request, and personal boundary.** This supports a respectful conversation and a boundary under the user's own control. It is not a jealousy-specific treatment and cannot be used to legitimise coercion, surveillance, threats, or stalking.

### 10.10 Rumination

**Schedule a deliberate review period.** Worry postponement is used in CBT for GAD. It is intended for repetitive, non-urgent hypothetical worry and must not delay medical, practical, or safety decisions. The 2025 transdiagnostic meta-analysis of 55 studies and 4,970 participants found an overall CBT effect of g = −0.67 on repetitive negative thinking; RNT-specific interventions showed a larger effect than less-specific approaches.

**Identify what rumination is trying to do.** This looks at beliefs about the function of repeated thinking, such as preparation, self-punishment, threat monitoring, or certainty-seeking. It is a process-focused reflection aid, not the full protocol of metacognitive therapy or rumination-focused CBT.

### 10.11 Low self-esteem

**Build a balanced self-evaluation continuum.** A continuum helps challenge all-or-nothing self-evaluation by defining observable anchors and placing specific evidence within a range. This is a recognised cognitive therapy procedure, but the tool is not a treatment programme by itself.

**Keep a positive and corrective data log.** Fennell-style CBT for low self-esteem uses a positive-data approach to collect specific experiences that do not fit a negative core belief. The log is not a generic affirmation list; it should include credible, concrete, balanced information.

### 10.12 Social anxiety

**Plan a fair video-feedback review.** Video feedback is a component of Clark and Wells CBT for social anxiety and may help compare feared self-imagery with observable behaviour. Recording must be purposeful, limited, and consent-based. This must not become repeated appearance checking, performance surveillance, or an excuse to record others without consent.

**Select one safety behaviour to test.** The tool asks the user to identify a subtle behaviour that may prevent new learning, write a prediction, and test a modest change in a safe interaction. This is aligned with the Clark and Wells model. It is not a generic exposure challenge and should be integrated with a structured protocol for clinically significant social anxiety.

### 10.13 Panic

**Identify panic-related safety behaviours.** This is a reflection aid to bring escape and safety behaviours into a panic-focused CBT formulation. It explicitly prohibits stopping medically necessary precautions and does not provide symptom-induction exercises.

**Create a balanced panic coping card.** Psychoeducation and coping statements are components of panic-focused CBT. The card must be based on an appropriate clinical assessment and must include when medical review or urgent care is needed. It cannot determine whether a new symptom is panic or a medical problem.

### 10.14 OCD

**Notice covert and mental rituals.** OCD compulsions may be mental as well as observable. The tool helps the user identify neutralising, reviewing, counting, or silent checking so the pattern can be discussed with an ERP-trained clinician. It is an assessment aid, not unsupervised response prevention.

**Map reassurance and accommodation patterns.** Family and close others can become involved in rituals or reassurance. The map is collaborative and clinician-guided; it must not be used to blame relatives or abruptly withdraw support.

### 10.15 Trauma reactions

**Use a present-time orientation cue.** Neutral sensory and contextual orientation can be a supportive grounding practice for some people. It is not a validated trauma-processing intervention and may not feel helpful for everyone; users are told to stop if it worsens distress or dissociation.

**Map triggers to support options.** This is practical support planning focused on current functioning, safety, and available help. It avoids trauma-memory writing, imaginal exposure, or unsupervised processing.

### 10.16 Sleep difficulties

**Review stimulus-control principles.** Stimulus control is a recognised CBT-I component. The app explains its purpose but directs users to a CBT-I clinician for the full procedure. It does not prescribe sleep restriction or make changes that could create unsafe sleepiness.

**Track sleep-related mental arousal.** Cognitive therapy is a component of multicomponent CBT-I. This record notices pressure thoughts, clock-checking, and sleep performance monitoring. It is not intended to replace CBT-I or turn sleep tracking into compulsive monitoring.

### 10.17 Perfectionism

**Set a proportionate checking limit.** This is a behavioural experiment in reducing unnecessary review on a low-consequence task. The tool excludes safety-critical, medical, legal, and other high-stakes work.

**Review a mistake as data.** The exercise separates correction and learning from global self-condemnation. It is a cognitive/behavioural review, not permission to ignore real consequences or accountability.

## 11. New Health Anxiety workspace: detailed clinical boundary

The Health Anxiety workspace is classified as a focused-care area, not a general emotion. Its intended audience is adults who have had appropriate medical assessment and recognise a health-anxiety pattern.

The three exercises are deliberately limited:

1. **Map the health-anxiety cycle** — trigger, feared interpretation, anxiety, checking/reassurance, and short-term relief.
2. **Review repeated checking and reassurance** — distinguish clinically advised monitoring from non-essential certainty-seeking; any change to checking is discussed with a clinician.
3. **Write a balanced health appraisal** — separate observation from catastrophic interpretation while preserving uncertainty and following the clinician's plan.

The site does not provide medical triage, symptom interpretation, disease probability calculators, reassurance about specific symptoms, or advice to discontinue tests or treatment. An appropriate medical-care plan takes precedence. New, severe, worsening, or urgent symptoms require medical assessment according to local services.

The evidence label applies to psychological treatment for health anxiety in adults, not to this website or these worksheets as stand-alone interventions.

## 12. Revised technique inventory and evidence discipline

The application now offers **18 workspaces and 88 interactive tools**. The tools are grouped by their function and target:

- **Cognitive methods:** thought records, responsibility reattribution, continuums, coping appraisals, balanced health appraisals, and core-belief data logs.
- **Behavioural methods:** activity monitoring, graded tasks, behavioural experiments, exposure-learning records, checking-limit experiments, and relapse-prevention plans.
- **Functional analysis:** anger chains, avoidance-cost maps, reassurance audits, rumination-function analysis, and trigger/support maps.
- **Condition-specific components:** Clark and Wells social-anxiety components, panic-focused CBT preparation, OCD mental-ritual assessment, trauma-support planning, and CBT-I education.
- **Communication and problem-solving:** assertive boundary statements, relationship requests, demand prioritisation, goal adjustment, and practical repair planning.

The increased number of tools does not imply 88 independently validated interventions. Many are different prompts that help users practise a smaller number of established CBT procedures in context. The product must continue to state the evidence status and boundaries at the point of use.

## 14. Updated maintenance requirements

For each future tool, the content review should record:

1. Whether the target is a common emotion, a transdiagnostic process, or a clinical condition.
2. The actual CBT model or protocol in which the technique appears.
3. Whether the evidence supports a whole treatment package or an individual component.
4. The population, comparator, outcomes, effect estimate, heterogeneity, and important risk-of-bias limitations when available.
5. What the exercise is not intended to do.
6. Whether it can be self-guided, requires clinician support, or should only be explained as psychoeducation.
7. Any physical, psychological, privacy, consent, or interpersonal safety restrictions.
8. Whether changes to the product and the scientific review have been committed together.

The scientific audit remains a targeted review rather than a formally registered PRISMA systematic review. New research should be added only after checking publication details, population, methods, and the interpretation of the actual outcomes. Abstracts and search snippets are discovery tools, not a substitute for critical appraisal of the full paper when accessible.
