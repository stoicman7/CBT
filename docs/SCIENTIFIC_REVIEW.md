# CBT Workspace — Scientific and Clinical Review

**Review date:** 23 November 2026  
**Audience:** Adults using an educational, self-guided skills workspace  
**Purpose:** Explain the evidence base, the limits of emotion-specific claims, and the safety boundaries applied to the application.

## Executive summary

CBT is not one worksheet or one universal method. It is a family of structured psychological interventions that link thoughts, attention, emotion, physiology, and behaviour. The most appropriate techniques depend on the problem formulation and, when a mental disorder is present, on the disorder-specific treatment protocol.

The application therefore uses two different navigation layers:

1. **Common emotional experiences and thinking patterns** such as anger, stress, frustration, guilt, shame, jealousy, rumination, and low self-esteem. These pages offer carefully labelled CBT-informed skills. A page in this group is not evidence that the emotion is a disorder or that a dedicated treatment protocol has been validated for it.
2. **Condition-focused areas** such as social anxiety, panic, OCD, trauma reactions, and chronic insomnia. These pages explain established treatment components but also make clear when a validated protocol and a trained practitioner are needed.

The evidence map is deliberately conservative. Strong evidence for a diagnosis-specific treatment does not automatically establish the efficacy of every exercise on a website. Likewise, evidence that a general skill is useful in CBT does not mean that the skill is a complete treatment.

The site is an educational and practice resource. It does not diagnose, assess clinical risk, prescribe treatment, provide crisis intervention, or replace a qualified mental-health professional.

## 1. Scope and method of this review

### 1.1 What was reviewed

A targeted evidence scan was conducted for the application build, prioritising:

- Current or maintained clinical practice guidelines, particularly NICE, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, and VA/DoD.
- Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of randomised clinical trials.
- Component analyses where they help distinguish the contribution of specific CBT methods.
- Established CBT treatment manuals and professional training materials for descriptions of standard techniques.
- Primary trials and systematic reviews for more specialised processes such as rumination-focused CBT.

The search covered the following domains: adult CBT generally; transdiagnostic CBT; anger and aggression; elevated and occupational stress; generalised anxiety; panic; social anxiety; depression and behavioural activation; low self-esteem; repetitive negative thinking and rumination; perfectionism; OCD and exposure with response prevention; PTSD and trauma-focused CBT; and chronic insomnia/CBT-I.

### 1.2 Important limitation

This document is an **evidence map and targeted scientific audit, not a PRISMA systematic review**. It does not claim to have retrieved every paper, searched every database, assessed every included trial independently, or completed a formal risk-of-bias appraisal for every study. Search access was based on available online sources and published abstracts/guidelines. Some primary papers may require institutional access.

Consequently:

- The evidence labels are qualitative navigation aids, not formal GRADE ratings.
- They describe the strength of support for the named clinical approach or method in the stated context, not a universal ranking of exercises.
- A meta-analysis result is reported with its population, intervention, and comparator in mind. It must not be generalised to every user or every emotional situation.
- References should be revisited as guidelines and evidence evolve.

### 1.3 Evidence-label definitions used in the product

| Label | Meaning in this product |
|---|---|
| Strong | A recognised clinical guideline or converging high-quality evidence supports a defined intervention for the named condition. This does not mean the page itself is a complete treatment. |
| Moderate | Systematic reviews or controlled trials support the approach, but effects, populations, or protocols vary, or important qualifications remain. |
| Foundational / evidence-informed | The exercise adapts an established CBT procedure, but direct evidence for the specific emotion as a stand-alone target is limited or not established. |
| Limited / specialist context | Evidence for a universal self-guided intervention is weak, indirect, or highly context-dependent. The page is cautious and should direct users to assessment when symptoms or risk warrant it. |

The letter-style marker in the interface is a visual shorthand for these categories only. It is not a clinical grade, a predicted outcome, or an assessment of an individual.

## 2. What the wider CBT evidence supports

### 2.1 CBT across mental disorders

A 2025 unified series of meta-analyses examined 375 trials comparing CBT with control conditions across adult mental disorders. The authors reported significant reductions across the disorders studied, with effect sizes varying by diagnosis; results were large for several conditions including major depression, panic disorder, social anxiety disorder, generalised anxiety disorder, OCD, and bulimia nervosa, and very large for PTSD and specific phobia. The authors also noted limitations in evidence quality. This broad review supports CBT as an important treatment family, but does not establish that one generic technique works equally well across conditions.

**Product implication:** use disorder-specific pages and avoid presenting one cognitive worksheet as a treatment for everything.

### 2.2 Transdiagnostic CBT

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis included 53 studies and 6,705 participants in trials of transdiagnostic CBT for emotional disorders. Compared with control conditions, the reported effects were g = 0.74 for depression and g = 0.77 for anxiety. The review found benefits at 3-, 6-, and 12-month follow-up, but not at 24 months. The authors reported heterogeneity in design and methodological quality.

**Product implication:** recurring processes such as avoidance, threat appraisal, repetitive negative thinking, and emotion regulation can be addressed across diagnoses, while preserving disorder-specific treatment requirements.

### 2.3 Emotion regulation as a transdiagnostic process

A 2026 systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis included 619 studies, 41,590 clinical participants, and 36,787 controls. It found broad differences in self-reported emotion-regulation difficulties across mental disorders, including greater rumination and avoidance and lower use of acceptance, problem-solving, and reappraisal. These are group-level associations, not evidence that any single online exercise treats a disorder.

**Product implication:** the site can teach emotion awareness, problem-solving, and reappraisal as general skills, but should not imply that emotion regulation is the sole mechanism or a universal cure.

## 3. Audit by emotional domain and condition

### 3.1 Anger

**Evidence base.** Saini's 2009 meta-analysis included 96 studies and 139 treatment effects across nine categories of psychological intervention. The overall weighted standardised mean difference was 0.76 (95% CI 0.67–0.85), with substantial heterogeneity (I² approximately 66%). The included treatments were not all CBT. A 2018 review of meta-analyses reported that cognitive-behavioural treatments were widely used and that anger interventions showed at least moderate effectiveness in non-clinical and psychiatric populations, while aggression outcomes were less consistent.

A 2024 meta-analytic review of anger-management activities included 154 studies, 184 independent samples, and 10,189 participants. Activities that reduced arousal—such as breathing, meditation, and relaxation-oriented practices—reduced anger and aggression (g = −0.63; 95% CI −0.82 to −0.43). Activities that increased arousal, including cathartic venting, did not reliably reduce anger.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Functional analysis of trigger, appraisal, bodily activation, urge, behaviour, and consequence.
- Cognitive reappraisal of assumptions about intent, disrespect, and threat.
- Problem-solving and assertive communication.
- Relaxation or other low-arousal strategies to create time for a deliberate response.
- Relapse planning around predictable triggers.

**What the evidence does not justify:** anger is not itself a psychiatric diagnosis; the meta-analytic effect across psychological interventions is not a CBT-only estimate; anger reduction does not automatically imply reduced violence; and a brief online exercise is not an intervention for domestic violence, stalking, threats, or imminent aggression.

**Product decision:** mark anger as moderate and variable. Include a chain analysis, low-arousal pause, and thought review. Explicitly prioritise distance and urgent help where there is a risk of harm.

### 3.2 Stress

**Evidence base.** A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of internet-delivered CBT for adults with elevated stress or stress-related disorders included 14 comparisons and 1,831 participants. It reported a pooled post-treatment effect on self-rated stress of d = 0.78 (95% CI 0.66–0.90). The eligible studies were targeted at elevated stress; this result should not be presented as proof that an app can resolve objectively unsafe or excessive demands.

A Cochrane review on occupational stress among healthcare workers included 58 studies, 54 of them randomised, with 7,188 participants. It found low-quality evidence that CBT with or without relaxation did not clearly reduce stress at one month, while effects favoured intervention at one to six months and beyond six months in a small number of studies. CBT did not clearly outperform an alternative intervention in the limited head-to-head comparisons. The result is context-specific and quality-limited.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Stressor mapping and practical problem-solving.
- Cognitive work on catastrophic predictions and rigid performance rules.
- Behavioural planning, pacing, and graded task management.
- Relaxation and recovery as adjunctive strategies.
- Boundary-setting and help-seeking.

**Product decision:** mark stress as evidence-supported with important context qualifications. The page must not imply that distress caused by unsafe work, poverty, discrimination, caregiving load, or abuse is simply a thinking error.

### 3.3 Generalised anxiety and repetitive worry

**Evidence base.** A 2024 systematic review and network meta-analysis examined 65 randomised trials with 5,048 adults with generalised anxiety disorder. CBT, third-wave CBTs, and relaxation therapy outperformed treatment as usual on effectiveness outcomes. When high-risk-of-bias studies were removed, CBT and third-wave approaches remained superior; CBT alone was associated with long-term effectiveness in that analysis. Treatment acceptability did not differ clearly from usual care.

NICE CG113 recommends a stepped-care model. For more marked impairment or inadequate response to lower-intensity options, it recommends a structured high-intensity intervention such as CBT or applied relaxation, delivered by trained and competent practitioners. The guideline describes a typical CBT course of 12–15 weekly sessions, with adaptation according to need.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Self-monitoring of worry and functional impact.
- Cognitive restructuring of threat predictions.
- Worry postponement and discrimination between solvable problems and hypothetical worry.
- Problem-solving for actionable concerns.
- Exposure and practice tolerating uncertainty.
- Reducing avoidance and reassurance-seeking where these maintain the cycle.

**Product decision:** mark anxiety as strong for several defined anxiety disorders, but clearly state that the product is not a GAD treatment protocol. Present worry postponement as a skills exercise, not as a replacement for structured care.

### 3.4 Panic

**Evidence base.** A component network meta-analysis of 72 studies and 4,064 participants examined which elements of CBT for panic disorder were associated with better outcomes. Interoceptive exposure and face-to-face delivery were associated with better efficacy and acceptability. The analysis found lower efficacy associations for muscle relaxation and virtual-reality exposure in the examined packages. An APA practice guideline describes psychoeducation, self-monitoring, cognitive restructuring, exposure to feared cues, modification of anxiety-maintaining behaviours, and relapse prevention as components of panic-focused CBT.

**Product decision:** explain the panic cycle and provide a reflective record. Do not provide instructions for users to induce breathlessness, dizziness, tachycardia, or other sensations. Interoceptive exposure requires clinical assessment, screening for relevant medical risks, and a planned protocol. New, severe, or unusual physical symptoms must not be self-diagnosed as panic.

### 3.5 Fear and specific phobia

**Evidence base.** Exposure is a central behavioural component in CBT for phobias and several anxiety disorders. It is planned contact with feared cues to support new learning, not a dare, punishment, or requirement to tolerate unsafe conditions. Clinical guidelines for anxiety and social anxiety describe graded exposure, behavioural experiments, and reduction of avoidance or safety behaviours as protocol components.

**Product decision:** offer a ladder-planning worksheet only for low-risk, ordinary fears. The product must distinguish real danger from anxiety and must not encourage exposure to unsafe situations, trauma reminders, or medically risky sensations. Severe, complex, or disabling phobias warrant a trained practitioner.

### 3.6 Depression and low mood

**Evidence base.** NICE NG222, published in 2022 and last reviewed on 30 January 2026, includes structured CBT and behavioural activation among treatment options for adults with depression. The guideline describes CBT as goal-oriented and structured, focused on the interaction of thoughts, beliefs, feelings, and behaviour. It also describes behavioural activation as a structured intervention focused on the relationship between activity and mood, including avoidance and withdrawal. The recommended clinical formats involve trained practitioners and structured sessions.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Activity monitoring and activity scheduling.
- Graded task assignment.
- Activities linked to pleasure, mastery, connection, and values.
- Cognitive restructuring of automatic thoughts and core beliefs.
- Problem-solving and relapse-prevention planning.

**Product decision:** mark the treatment approaches as strongly supported for depression, but do not diagnose depression from a mood check-in. The app uses small activity-planning exercises and warns that persistent, severe, or risky symptoms need assessment.

### 3.7 Frustration

Frustration is a common emotional response to blocked goals, difficult tasks, and mismatched expectations. It is not a single psychiatric disorder with one validated CBT protocol.

**CBT-derived components:** ABC-style functional analysis, cognitive flexibility, problem-solving, graded task assignment, and behavioural experiments.

**Product decision:** label the content foundational/evidence-informed. The application must not claim that the three exercises are a validated frustration treatment programme.

### 3.8 Guilt

Guilt can be proportionate and motivate repair, or it can become persistent and excessive. It may occur in depression, OCD, trauma-related disorders, and other presentations. Responsibility appraisal and cognitive restructuring are used in relevant protocols, but there is no universal online guilt protocol suitable for all causes.

**CBT-derived components:** distinguish facts from hindsight, estimate responsibility proportionately, identify realistic repair, and reduce repetitive unproductive review.

**Product decision:** label as foundational. Trauma-related guilt, obsessional doubt, and severe depression require condition-specific assessment rather than repeated self-help disputation.

### 3.9 Shame

Shame can involve global negative self-evaluation, concealment, and avoidance. It is addressed in different therapies and may be part of trauma-focused treatment. The evidence does not justify presenting one generic worksheet as a validated stand-alone treatment for shame.

**CBT-derived components:** replace global labels with specific descriptions, test social predictions using carefully chosen behavioural experiments, and use fair evidence-based reappraisal.

**Product decision:** label as foundational/limited as a stand-alone target. Do not use reappraisal to minimise abuse, discrimination, exploitation, or another person's harmful behaviour. Trauma-related shame belongs in appropriate trauma-focused care.

### 3.10 Jealousy

Jealousy can reflect uncertainty, realistic relationship information, attachment concerns, perceived threat, or patterns of coercive control. It is not one diagnosis with a universal evidence-based CBT protocol. A generic cognitive exercise must not be used to invalidate credible evidence of betrayal or abuse.

**CBT-derived components:** distinguish observation from inference, identify reassurance/checking loops, and use respectful communication and personal boundaries.

**Product decision:** label as limited. The product explicitly prohibits using the exercises to justify tracking, surveillance, account access, threats, interrogation, isolation, or coercion. Delusional jealousy, stalking, violence, and coercive control require professional assessment and safety planning.

### 3.11 Rumination and repetitive negative thinking

**Evidence base.** A 2025 transdiagnostic meta-analysis included 55 studies and 4,970 participants. CBT interventions reduced repetitive negative thinking compared with control groups, with an overall post-treatment effect of g = −0.67. RNT-specific interventions showed a larger effect (g = −0.99) than less specific approaches (g = −0.56). A 2024 systematic review of rumination-focused CBT included 12 studies, 10 of them randomised trials, and described promising but preliminary evidence, while calling for more rigorous trials. Earlier randomised research tested rumination-focused CBT as an adjunct for residual depression.

**CBT components with a rationale:**

- Identify when thinking has stopped producing new information.
- Shift from abstract self-focused questions to specific, concrete processing.
- Separate actionable problems from hypothetical uncertainty and unchangeable past events.
- Use behavioural activation and planned attention shifts.
- Consider protocol-based rumination-focused CBT for persistent depressive rumination.

**Product decision:** mark as moderate, with a note that process-specific approaches are promising and protocol evidence is developing. Do not instruct users to suppress thoughts or assume that every repeated thought is pathological.

### 3.12 Low self-esteem

**Evidence base.** A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis examined CBT interventions based on the Fennell model of low self-esteem. The review reported promising changes in self-reported self-esteem, while noting uncertainty about whether these interventions differ from those targeting depression.

**CBT components with a rationale:** identify core beliefs and conditional rules, gather balanced evidence, test beliefs through behavioural experiments, and reduce overcompensation or avoidance.

**Product decision:** mark as moderate/promising rather than definitive. Avoid simplistic affirmations and avoid interpreting external mistreatment as merely a negative belief.

### 3.13 Social anxiety

**Evidence base.** NICE CG159 recommends individual CBT based on established models, including the Clark and Wells model and the Heimberg model. The Clark and Wells protocol includes work on self-focused attention, safety-seeking behaviours, video feedback, externally focused attention, behavioural experiments, core beliefs, and pre- and post-event processing. The Heimberg model includes cognitive restructuring and graduated exposure. These are structured treatment protocols, not simply exposure challenges.

**Product decision:** mark as strong for disorder-specific CBT. Explain components, but make clear that a full protocol should be delivered by a trained practitioner. Behavioural experiments should be planned and reviewed, not used to force social exposure.

### 3.14 OCD and exposure with response prevention

**Evidence base.** NICE CG31 recommends CBT that includes exposure and response prevention for adults with OCD, with treatment intensity and format adapted to impairment and preference. It notes exposure to obsessive thoughts and prevention of mental rituals or neutralising strategies when compulsions are not overt. It also emphasises appropriate practitioner training.

**Product decision:** mark as strong for ERP as a specialised treatment. The site explains the obsession–compulsion cycle and encourages specialist support. It does not provide an unsupervised ERP hierarchy or encourage repeated debate with intrusive thoughts. Reassurance can itself become a compulsion.

### 3.15 PTSD and trauma-related reactions

**Evidence base.** NICE NG116 recommends validated individual trauma-focused CBT interventions for adults with PTSD or clinically important symptoms, including cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy for PTSD, narrative exposure therapy, and prolonged exposure. It describes a structured course, commonly 8–12 sessions, delivered by trained practitioners with ongoing supervision. Treatment includes safety planning, managing arousal and flashbacks, processing trauma-related meanings, and reducing avoidance. Supported computerised trauma-focused CBT is limited to selected circumstances and includes exclusions for severe symptoms and risk of harm.

**Product decision:** mark trauma-focused CBT as strong for the indicated population but do not turn this site into a trauma-processing tool. No self-guided trauma-memory writing, imaginal exposure, or trauma narrative exercise is provided. The trauma page focuses on safety, education, support, and finding trained care.

### 3.16 Chronic insomnia and CBT-I

**Evidence base.** The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's 2021 clinical practice guideline gives a strong recommendation for multicomponent CBT-I in adults with chronic insomnia. Its recommendation was based on a substantial evidence base, including many randomised trials. The guideline gives conditional support to some single components such as stimulus control and sleep restriction, and advises against sleep hygiene as the sole treatment. The 2025 VA/DoD chronic insomnia guideline also recommends CBT-I.

**Product decision:** mark CBT-I as strong and condition-specific. Offer sleep observation and cognitive reflection, not a do-it-yourself sleep-restriction prescription. Sleep restriction can be inappropriate or risky for some conditions and safety-sensitive circumstances and should be discussed with a qualified clinician.

### 3.17 Perfectionism

**Evidence base.** A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis examined randomised controlled trials of CBT for perfectionism. This supports targeted CBT programmes for unhelpful perfectionism, although it does not mean that high standards or conscientiousness are pathological.

**CBT components with a rationale:** identify conditional rules, test feared consequences of mistakes, reduce unnecessary checking, and practise flexible standards through low-risk behavioural experiments.

**Product decision:** mark as moderate. All experiments must be limited to low-consequence tasks; no lowering standards in safety-critical, legal, medical, or other high-stakes work.

### 4.1 Cognitive restructuring — correct use

A sound thought record should:

1. Describe a specific situation.
2. Identify the automatic thought, image, or prediction.
3. Name the emotion and, when useful, rate its intensity.
4. Identify evidence supporting the thought.
5. Identify evidence that complicates it or has been overlooked.
6. Produce a balanced and credible alternative.
7. Choose a proportionate next action.

The aim is not to make every thought positive. A meta-analytic review of cognitive restructuring and psychotherapy outcome reported a positive process–outcome association, but the analysis included only four studies and 353 clients. The result is encouraging, but it is not proof that completing a digital worksheet alone causes clinical recovery.

### 4.2 Exposure — correct use

Exposure should be collaborative, graded, specific, and based on a formulation. The user should know the target prediction and what new learning is being sought. Exposure is not flooding, punishment, forced confrontation, or entering an objectively dangerous situation.

The application does not provide self-directed interoceptive exposure for panic, trauma-memory exposure, or full OCD ERP instructions.

### 4.3 Behavioural activation — correct use

Behavioural activation is not simply a list of pleasant activities. It involves monitoring the relationship between activity and mood, identifying avoidance, planning actions linked to meaning or reinforcement, grading tasks, and reviewing what happened. Motivation may not come first.

### 4.4 Problem-solving — correct use

Structured problem-solving is most appropriate when at least part of the problem can be influenced. The workflow is: define the problem, define a realistic goal, generate options, compare options, select a first step, and review. Repeatedly trying to solve an uncontrollable uncertainty can become rumination.

### 4.5 Relaxation and anger

Arousal-reduction strategies can be useful when high activation makes deliberate action difficult. The anger meta-analysis of arousal-related activities does not mean every relaxation method is a complete CBT treatment. It also cautions against equating venting or aggressive discharge with regulation.

## 5. Safety, escalation, and ethical boundaries

### 5.1 Scope of intended users

The product is written for adults. It is not a child or adolescent treatment tool and has not been clinically validated as a digital therapeutic.

### 5.2 Situations requiring professional or urgent support

The application should direct users towards qualified local support when symptoms are persistent, worsening, or interfere substantially with daily life. Urgent emergency support is appropriate when a person may act on thoughts of harming themselves or someone else, cannot remain safe, is in immediate danger, or is experiencing a psychiatric or medical emergency.

The product cannot identify a local crisis service without knowing the user's jurisdiction. It should therefore tell the user to contact local emergency services or a local crisis service and, where safe, a trusted person.

### 5.3 Specific boundaries

- No diagnosis or diagnostic score is produced.
- No medication recommendations are made.
- No trauma-memory processing is provided.
- No do-it-yourself panic-sensation induction is provided.
- No unsupervised full OCD/ERP protocol is provided.
- No sleep-restriction prescription is provided.
- No advice legitimises violence, coercion, surveillance, stalking, or privacy invasion.
- Exercises must not reframe genuine abuse, discrimination, unsafe work, or structural adversity as merely distorted thinking.
- Users should stop an exercise that increases distress substantially and seek appropriate support.

### 5.4 Why these boundaries matter

Clinical guidelines describe treatment protocols delivered by trained, competent, and supervised practitioners for several conditions. A self-guided website cannot reproduce the therapeutic relationship, individual formulation, risk assessment, medical screening, adjustment, or supervision built into those protocols.

## 6. Privacy and implementation audit

The current implementation is a static, browser-based application.

- No account, server-side database, analytics, or external AI service is required.
- Worksheet notes, check-ins, and plan completion are stored in browser local storage.
- Data remain on the current browser profile unless the user exports them.
- Users can export a JSON copy or clear local data.
- Local storage is not encrypted. Anyone with access to the same browser profile or device may be able to read saved entries.
- Private browsing, clearing site data, or switching browsers may remove access to saved notes.
- Users should avoid saving sensitive material on a shared or monitored device.
- External research references open in a new tab and are not used to transmit journal content.

The site imports no third-party application runtime. The stylesheet currently uses Google Fonts as a visual enhancement; system-font fallbacks preserve readability when the font service is unavailable. No personal journal content is sent to the font provider.

## 8. Maintenance requirements

Before adding a new emotion or technique:

1. Define whether the target is a common emotion, a transdiagnostic process, or a recognised clinical condition.
2. Identify the specific treatment protocol and population, rather than citing CBT in general.
3. Prefer current guidelines, systematic reviews, and randomised trials.
4. Record sample, comparator, outcome, effect estimate, and limitations when reporting quantitative findings.

6. Add clear exclusions, practitioner requirements, and safety boundaries.
7. Update this review and the visible evidence note together.

**Bottom line:** the platform should make high-quality CBT skills easier to understand and practise while being honest about what has been validated, what remains an evidence-informed adaptation, and when self-guided work is not appropriate.

# Supplementary audit — expanded toolkit and new evidence (23 November 2026)

This section documents the second development audit. It evaluates the additional exercises added after the first release, records newly reviewed evidence, and states where the additional tools are adaptations rather than validated stand-alone treatments.

## 9. New evidence reviewed

### 9.1 Anger and emotion-regulation strategies (2025)

Pop et al. (2025) conducted a meta-analysis of 81 studies and 115 effect sizes examining associations between anger and emotion-regulation strategies. Anger was positively associated with avoidance, rumination, and suppression, and negatively associated with acceptance and cognitive reappraisal. Heterogeneity was large, and the included studies differed in anger type, clinical status, criminal status, culture, study quality, and sample composition.

**Interpretation:** this is an association meta-analysis, not a trial demonstrating that a particular worksheet or strategy causes anger reduction. It supports paying attention to rumination, avoidance, suppression, acceptance, and reappraisal in anger formulations, but it does not establish a universal recipe.

**Product changes:** the anger workspace now includes an anger-rumination interruption record and a structured assertive-boundary planner. The latter is a CBT-compatible communication skill, not an intervention for violence, coercive control, or imminent risk. The earlier 2024 meta-analysis of arousal-changing activities remains relevant: arousal-decreasing activities reduced anger/aggression on average (g = −0.63), whereas arousal-increasing activities did not show an overall benefit (g = −0.02). Venting should not be prescribed as an anger-management technique.

### 9.2 Behavioural activation for depression (2026)

Cuijpers et al. (2026) included 105 trials and 13,933 patients in a comprehensive meta-analysis. For adult outpatients, behavioural activation compared with control conditions produced an SMD of 0.67 (95% CI 0.54–0.80; 61 comparisons), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 71%) and a broad prediction interval. There was no significant difference between behavioural activation and other therapies in the included head-to-head comparisons. Self-guided behavioural activation also showed a smaller significant effect (SMD 0.36; 15 comparisons).

**Interpretation:** behavioural activation is an evidence-supported depression treatment, but effects vary and a self-guided activity worksheet is not equivalent to a complete behavioural activation protocol.

**Product changes:** the low-mood workspace now includes activity/mood/mastery monitoring and an early-warning/relapse-prevention plan. The latter is also consistent with NICE NG222 recommendations to identify warning signs, stressors, avoidance, and rumination and to make concrete contingency plans after improvement.

### 9.3 CBT-I components and delivery (2024)

Furukawa et al. (2024) analysed 241 trials involving 31,452 participants. The component network meta-analysis found associations with improved remission for cognitive restructuring (incremental OR 1.68), third-wave components (1.49), sleep restriction (1.49), and stimulus control (1.43). Sleep-hygiene education was not identified as an essential component in that analysis (iOR 1.01). The point estimate for relaxation was below one, but its confidence interval crossed one; it should not be interpreted as proof that relaxation is always harmful. The authors noted that possible component interactions could affect conclusions.

**Interpretation:** chronic insomnia treatment should not be reduced to sleep-hygiene tips. Multicomponent CBT-I is recommended by clinical guidelines; individual components need to be selected and sequenced within a programme suitable for the person.

**Product changes:** the sleep workspace now contains a stimulus-control review and sleep-related mental-arousal record. Both are educational/reflection tools. The site still does not calculate or prescribe sleep-restriction windows.

### 9.4 Health-anxiety psychological interventions (2026)

Lai et al. (2026), published online on 22 May 2026 in the *British Journal of Psychiatry*, conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis of 35 trials involving 3,263 adults with substantial health anxiety. CBT, exposure therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy, metacognitive therapy, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, and behavioural stress management showed significant effects compared with a waiting-list control. Component analysis linked exposure and response prevention, cognitive restructuring, and mindfulness with improved outcomes. The authors concluded that CBT and third-wave CBT are reasonable first-line choices for health anxiety.

**Interpretation:** the network comparison is not a licence to dismiss physical symptoms. The component analysis identifies associations within treatment packages; it does not prove that a self-guided exercise is sufficient or that each component is independently effective for every patient. Medical assessment and appropriate follow-up remain essential.

**Product changes:** a dedicated Health Anxiety workspace has been added. Its exercises map the anxiety cycle, distinguish medically advised monitoring from non-essential repeated checking, and support balanced appraisal after appropriate assessment. The page explicitly prohibits using the exercises to ignore new or worsening symptoms, cancel recommended tests, stop treatment, or delay urgent medical care.

## 10. Audit of the 34 additional tools

The added methods below expand the range of CBT skills represented in the platform. A technique can be a recognised CBT procedure without being an independently validated stand-alone treatment for the emotion to which it is applied.

### 10.1 Anger

**Interrupt anger rumination.** This tool targets repeated mental replay after an anger-provoking event. Its structure asks whether replay is generating new information, a repair action, or merely renewed activation. The rationale is supported by the 2025 anger/emotion-regulation association meta-analysis and by the transdiagnostic RNT meta-analysis. It should not be used to suppress a legitimate grievance or avoid a necessary conversation.

**Prepare an assertive boundary statement.** This converts blame or an aggressive urge into a behaviour-specific description, effect, request, and personal boundary. It is a CBT-compatible communication and problem-solving skill. It must only be used where direct communication is safe; leaving, distancing, or seeking help takes priority where intimidation or violence is possible.

### 10.2 Stress

**Sort demands by urgency and influence.** A demand-prioritisation record helps distinguish genuine deadlines and consequences from a subjective sense that every task is equally urgent. It is a structured problem-solving adaptation, not a validated stress treatment by itself. It must not imply that unsafe workloads or structural adversity can be fixed by individual organisation.

**Build an early-warning and response plan.** This transfers a recognised maintenance-planning principle into stress self-management: identify early signs, likely contexts, practical adjustments, support requests, and escalation steps. It is not a validated stress-relapse scale. Occupational support is appropriate when the source of stress is unsafe or unreasonable work.

### 10.3 Anxiety

**Audit reassurance and checking.** The exercise examines the short-term relief and longer-term persistence associated with repeated checking. This can be relevant to anxiety formulations, but checking may be a compulsion in OCD. Therefore, the tool explicitly directs users with severe or compulsive symptoms to an OCD-informed clinician and does not provide an ERP hierarchy.

**Test a coping prediction.** Cognitive work often addresses both the probability of a feared outcome and beliefs about its consequences or manageability. This exercise asks what coping resources and realistic choices would remain if the feared event occurred. It is not an assurance that the event will not happen.

### 10.4 Fear

**Record what exposure teaches.** Exposure learning should be assessed against a specific prediction and observable outcome, not simply by whether anxiety falls during the exercise. The record captures the prediction, step, observed outcome, and learning. It is restricted to safe and appropriate approach work; trauma processing and interoceptive panic exposure remain outside the self-guided tool.

**Map the cost of avoidance.** Functional analysis makes the immediate relief and longer-term costs of avoidance visible. The tool distinguishes fear-driven avoidance from reasonable precautions. It never instructs a person to approach a genuine hazard.

### 10.5 Low mood

**Track activity, mood, and mastery.** Activity monitoring is a core behavioural-activation procedure. Recording pleasure and mastery separately can help identify reinforcing activities and patterns of withdrawal. The 2026 behavioural-activation meta-analysis supports behavioural activation as a treatment for adult depression, but the site worksheet is an entry-level practice aid, not the whole treatment.

**Create a low-mood early-warning plan.** This helps identify personal warning signs and actions to restart when symptoms recur. NICE NG222 explicitly describes relapse-prevention skills such as reviewing what helped, identifying triggers and early signs, and making contingency plans. If symptoms are persistent, severe, or associated with self-harm risk, assessment and care take priority over this worksheet.

### 10.6 Frustration

**Adjust the goal without abandoning it.** This separates a valued outcome from one inflexible method, deadline, or route. It uses cognitive flexibility and practical problem-solving. It is evidence-informed rather than a validated frustration-specific treatment.

**Pause before acting on the urge.** This maps the need under an impulsive response—such as rest, information, help, or a revised plan—and asks for a proportionate next step. It is a CBT-informed response-planning skill, not a treatment for aggression or impulse-control disorders.

### 10.7 Guilt

**Draw a responsibility map.** Responsibility reattribution can help when a person takes total responsibility for an outcome involving several actors or circumstances. The worksheet prompts the user to consider information available at the time and to avoid hindsight bias. It is not suitable for self-directed checking when responsibility fears are part of OCD.

**Separate repair from self-punishment.** The tool distinguishes a specific, proportionate action from repetitive self-criticism after reasonable repair. It must not minimise real harm, legal responsibilities, or obligations to make amends.

### 10.8 Shame

**Replace the all-or-nothing self-judgement.** Continuum methods are used in cognitive therapy to move from global labels to more specific, evidence-based evaluations. Their use here is an adaptation; the site does not claim a validated shame-specific protocol.

**Examine the cost of hiding.** This explores whether concealment is a legitimate privacy or safety choice or whether shame-driven withdrawal is limiting support and valued activity. Disclosure remains optional. Users are never instructed to reveal sensitive details or disclose to unsafe people.

### 10.9 Jealousy

**Plan a response to the checking urge.** Functional analysis helps distinguish necessary information-gathering from repeated reassurance or checking aimed at certainty. The exercise prohibits accessing private accounts, devices, messages, or location data without consent.

**Clarify trust, request, and personal boundary.** This supports a respectful conversation and a boundary under the user's own control. It is not a jealousy-specific treatment and cannot be used to legitimise coercion, surveillance, threats, or stalking.

### 10.10 Rumination

**Schedule a deliberate review period.** Worry postponement is used in CBT for GAD. It is intended for repetitive, non-urgent hypothetical worry and must not delay medical, practical, or safety decisions. The 2025 transdiagnostic meta-analysis of 55 studies and 4,970 participants found an overall CBT effect of g = −0.67 on repetitive negative thinking; RNT-specific interventions showed a larger effect than less-specific approaches.

**Identify what rumination is trying to do.** This looks at beliefs about the function of repeated thinking, such as preparation, self-punishment, threat monitoring, or certainty-seeking. It is a process-focused reflection aid, not the full protocol of metacognitive therapy or rumination-focused CBT.

### 10.11 Low self-esteem

**Build a balanced self-evaluation continuum.** A continuum helps challenge all-or-nothing self-evaluation by defining observable anchors and placing specific evidence within a range. This is a recognised cognitive therapy procedure, but the tool is not a treatment programme by itself.

**Keep a positive and corrective data log.** Fennell-style CBT for low self-esteem uses a positive-data approach to collect specific experiences that do not fit a negative core belief. The log is not a generic affirmation list; it should include credible, concrete, balanced information.

### 10.12 Social anxiety

**Plan a fair video-feedback review.** Video feedback is a component of Clark and Wells CBT for social anxiety and may help compare feared self-imagery with observable behaviour. Recording must be purposeful, limited, and consent-based. This must not become repeated appearance checking, performance surveillance, or an excuse to record others without consent.

**Select one safety behaviour to test.** The tool asks the user to identify a subtle behaviour that may prevent new learning, write a prediction, and test a modest change in a safe interaction. This is aligned with the Clark and Wells model. It is not a generic exposure challenge and should be integrated with a structured protocol for clinically significant social anxiety.

### 10.13 Panic

**Identify panic-related safety behaviours.** This is a reflection aid to bring escape and safety behaviours into a panic-focused CBT formulation. It explicitly prohibits stopping medically necessary precautions and does not provide symptom-induction exercises.

**Create a balanced panic coping card.** Psychoeducation and coping statements are components of panic-focused CBT. The card must be based on an appropriate clinical assessment and must include when medical review or urgent care is needed. It cannot determine whether a new symptom is panic or a medical problem.

### 10.14 OCD

**Notice covert and mental rituals.** OCD compulsions may be mental as well as observable. The tool helps the user identify neutralising, reviewing, counting, or silent checking so the pattern can be discussed with an ERP-trained clinician. It is an assessment aid, not unsupervised response prevention.

**Map reassurance and accommodation patterns.** Family and close others can become involved in rituals or reassurance. The map is collaborative and clinician-guided; it must not be used to blame relatives or abruptly withdraw support.

### 10.15 Trauma reactions

**Use a present-time orientation cue.** Neutral sensory and contextual orientation can be a supportive grounding practice for some people. It is not a validated trauma-processing intervention and may not feel helpful for everyone; users are told to stop if it worsens distress or dissociation.

**Map triggers to support options.** This is practical support planning focused on current functioning, safety, and available help. It avoids trauma-memory writing, imaginal exposure, or unsupervised processing.

### 10.16 Sleep difficulties

**Review stimulus-control principles.** Stimulus control is a recognised CBT-I component. The app explains its purpose but directs users to a CBT-I clinician for the full procedure. It does not prescribe sleep restriction or make changes that could create unsafe sleepiness.

**Track sleep-related mental arousal.** Cognitive therapy is a component of multicomponent CBT-I. This record notices pressure thoughts, clock-checking, and sleep performance monitoring. It is not intended to replace CBT-I or turn sleep tracking into compulsive monitoring.

### 10.17 Perfectionism

**Set a proportionate checking limit.** This is a behavioural experiment in reducing unnecessary review on a low-consequence task. The tool excludes safety-critical, medical, legal, and other high-stakes work.

**Review a mistake as data.** The exercise separates correction and learning from global self-condemnation. It is a cognitive/behavioural review, not permission to ignore real consequences or accountability.

## 11. New Health Anxiety workspace: detailed clinical boundary

The Health Anxiety workspace is classified as a focused-care area, not a general emotion. Its intended audience is adults who have had appropriate medical assessment and recognise a health-anxiety pattern.

The three exercises are deliberately limited:

1. **Map the health-anxiety cycle** — trigger, feared interpretation, anxiety, checking/reassurance, and short-term relief.
2. **Review repeated checking and reassurance** — distinguish clinically advised monitoring from non-essential certainty-seeking; any change to checking is discussed with a clinician.
3. **Write a balanced health appraisal** — separate observation from catastrophic interpretation while preserving uncertainty and following the clinician's plan.

The site does not provide medical triage, symptom interpretation, disease probability calculators, reassurance about specific symptoms, or advice to discontinue tests or treatment. An appropriate medical-care plan takes precedence. New, severe, worsening, or urgent symptoms require medical assessment according to local services.

The evidence label applies to psychological treatment for health anxiety in adults, not to this website or these worksheets as stand-alone interventions.

# Third audit — broad CBT technique coverage and clinical-domain expansion

**Audit date:** 23 November 2026  
**Purpose:** Expand the first two reviews into a broad map of common CBT procedures, specialist protocol components, and related third-wave therapies; identify gaps in the application; and document the additional workspaces and catalogue entries.

## 15. Scope, search strategy, and limits of the phrase “all CBT tools”

### 15.1 Why there is no single closed list

There is no universally accepted, finite master list of every CBT tool. “CBT” refers to a family of cognitive and behavioural treatments, and the techniques used depend on the clinical problem, the explanatory formulation, treatment phase, and manual being followed. A behavioural experiment in social anxiety is not identical to exposure and response prevention in OCD, and neither is interchangeable with sleep-restriction therapy in CBT-I. Some manuals also describe therapist-delivery skills—such as collaborative agenda setting, feedback, and guided discovery—which are not stand-alone patient worksheets.

For this audit, “broad CBT coverage” therefore means:

- the principal procedures commonly described in cognitive therapy and behavioural treatment manuals;
- the important components identified in current disorder-specific clinical guidelines;
- commonly used condition-specific protocols where there is a meaningful evidence base;
- relevant behavioural and cognitive methods used in specialist settings;
- related third-wave therapies, clearly labelled as related approaches rather than classical CBT.

The result is a broad, curated catalogue, not a claim to have enumerated every exercise ever used in every CBT manual or research trial.

### 15.2 Search and source strategy

The audit used a targeted online scoping search, with searches organised around technique names, clinical conditions, and evidence type. Sources were prioritised in this order:

1. Clinical practice guidelines and official treatment recommendations (NICE, AASM, VA/DoD, government clinical guidance).
2. Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and component network meta-analyses.
3. Randomised clinical trials when they directly clarify a protocol or its outcomes.
4. Established cognitive therapy manuals and professional training materials for technique definitions and delivery principles.
5. Professional or educational resources for discovering additional worksheet terminology, not as the sole evidence basis for a treatment claim.

Search clusters included: Beckian cognitive therapy; guided discovery and Socratic dialogue; automatic-thought and core-belief work; behavioural experiments; exposure and response prevention; behavioural activation; problem-solving; graded tasks; habit reversal; insomnia treatment; social anxiety; panic; OCD; trauma-focused CBT; depression; eating-disorder-focused CBT; chronic pain; adult ADHD; substance-use disorders; prolonged grief; psychosis; and metacognitive/third-wave approaches.

A direct review of the Beck Institute Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale Manual was especially useful for distinguishing therapist-delivery skills from client exercises. The manual describes agenda-setting, feedback, collaboration, guided discovery, conceptualisation of key cognitions and behaviours, strategy for change, cognitive and behavioural methods, and homework. It also describes imagery, role-play, reattribution, graded tasks, activity scheduling, mastery/pleasure ratings, and cognitive rehearsal.

### 15.3 What this audit did not do

This is a targeted scoping audit, not a PRISMA-registered systematic review. It does not claim:

- to have exhaustively searched every database (including every PsycINFO, Embase, CINAHL, Scopus, and trial-register record);
- to have retrieved every manual or every proprietary treatment workbook;
- to have independently re-rated risk of bias for all underlying trials;
- to have compared all techniques head-to-head;
- to have established that each individual technique is effective as a stand-alone treatment.

The directory is intended to be comprehensive across principal, established technique families, but it remains a curated map. A procedure's presence in a treatment manual does not by itself establish its independent efficacy. An effect for a whole treatment package cannot be attributed to every component without component-specific evidence.

### 15.4 Evidence-label discipline

The application continues to use qualitative labels:

- **Strong:** a current guideline or converging, replicated evidence supports a defined treatment for a defined population. It does not mean this website is itself validated or that every listed exercise is a treatment.
- **Moderate:** evidence supports the approach, but study quality, population, outcome, delivery, or comparator differences require qualification.
- **Foundational / evidence-informed:** the entry adapts an established CBT procedure, but direct treatment evidence for that precise emotional target or worksheet is limited.
- **Limited / specialist context:** evidence is indirect, mixed, or highly context-dependent; the page should remain cautious and direct people towards assessment where appropriate.

For condition pages, the label refers to the treatment approach in its proper clinical context, not to the website's self-guided content.

### 16.2 Behavioural procedures

- behavioural experiments;
- graded in-vivo exposure;

- response prevention / ERP (specialist protocol);
- stimulus control;

- assertiveness training;

- applied relaxation;
- chronic-pain activity pacing;
- craving and high-risk-situation coping;
- regular-eating structure within CBT-ED.

The technique directory separates common behavioural skills from specialist protocols. A technique's existence in a manual does not mean that a self-guided web page should instruct users to perform it without assessment.

### 16.3 Therapist-delivery and treatment-maintenance procedures

The Beck Institute manual makes clear that cognitive therapy is not just a collection of worksheets. It also relies on collaborative process and treatment structure. The catalogue therefore now explicitly includes:

- psychoeducation and shared formulation;
- collaborative agenda and target setting;
- session feedback and summary;
- between-session practice and homework;
- progress review and collaborative feedback;
- relapse-prevention and maintenance planning.

Some of these are therapist-delivery processes. They are included to make the reference directory scientifically representative, but they should not be presented as individual self-help interventions that can reproduce psychotherapy.

### 16.4 Related therapies are labelled separately

The audit found several terms that are often grouped loosely under “modern CBT” but belong to distinct treatment models or developments. The directory labels these separately:

- **Metacognitive therapy:** Attention Training Technique and detached mindfulness.
- **Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT):** cognitive defusion, acceptance-related processes, values clarification, and committed action.
- **Dialectical behaviour therapy (DBT):** distress-tolerance skills.
- **Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT):** mindfulness and decentering.

These approaches overlap historically and conceptually with cognitive-behavioural traditions, and some third-wave components may be incorporated into transdiagnostic CBT packages. However, their theoretical models and treatment protocols are not identical to classical Beckian cognitive therapy. The catalogue should never imply that an ACT, DBT, MCT, or MBCT procedure is simply another name for a classic CBT worksheet.

43. **Session feedback and summary:** checks understanding, captures key learning, and agrees on the next step.

### 17.6 Related approaches (6 methods)

44. **Attention Training Technique (MCT).**
45. **Detached mindfulness (MCT).**
46. **Cognitive defusion (ACT).**
47. **Values clarification and committed action (ACT).**
48. **Distress-tolerance skills (DBT).**
49. **Mindfulness and decentering (MBCT).**

The directory distinguishes the six families because therapist-delivery processes, core cognitive methods, behavioural procedures, and specialist protocols are not interchangeable. It also keeps ACT, DBT, MCT, and MBCT separate from classical CBT, while recognising their historical and clinical relationships.


## 18. New specialist domains added to the application

Seven workspaces have been added. These broaden the clinical map beyond the original emotional difficulties and make clear when self-guided support is not an adequate substitute for treatment.

### 18.1 Chronic pain

**Guideline context.** NICE NG193 recommends considering CBT for chronic primary pain in people aged 16 and over, delivered by healthcare professionals with appropriate training. The guideline rationale reports that most of the evidence suggested benefits for quality of life, while consistent benefits were not demonstrated across all outcomes. The committee judged the evidence quality insufficient for a stronger recommendation.

**New tools:**

- pain, activity, and functional-impact mapping;
- sustainable activity-rhythm review;
- flare-up coping-plan preparation;
- pain-related prediction review.

**Boundary:** CBT for pain does not mean pain is imaginary. The tools must not be used to ignore new symptoms, cancel investigations, stop treatment, or push through medical warning signs. Pain pacing and activity changes should be individualised with healthcare professionals.

### 18.2 Adult ADHD skills

**Guideline context.** NICE NG87 states that adults with ADHD who need non-pharmacological treatment should be offered a structured supportive psychological intervention focused on ADHD, with regular follow-up; treatment may involve elements or a full course of CBT. The guideline's evidence review identifies psychoeducation, attention, organisation, planning, cognitive restructuring, and, where relevant, procrastination, frustration, anger, and communication skills.

**New tools:**

- one external task-capture system;
- low-friction task-start planning;
- estimated-versus-actual time review;
- distraction capture and return planning.

**Boundary:** the tools do not diagnose ADHD or replace assessment, medication review, accommodations, or structured psychological care. Difficulties are not framed as laziness or lack of character.

### 18.3 Body-focused repetitive behaviours (BFRBs)

**Evidence context.** A 2026 meta-analysis of psychotherapeutic and pharmacological interventions for trichotillomania included 29 trials in standard meta-analysis and 30 in network meta-analysis. The authors found strong replicated support for behavioural therapy with habit reversal, ACT-enhanced HRT, and N-acetylcysteine; interventions containing habit reversal generally performed better than those without it. This does not mean that the evidence is equally strong for every BFRB or every delivery format.

**New tools:**

- awareness-training record;
- environmental stimulus-control review;
- competing-response planning;
- lapse review without punishment.

**Boundary:** these are introductory preparation tools, not a full HRT programme. The exact competing response should be safe and individually selected. Painful restraints, injurious barriers, and self-punishment are prohibited. Skin injury, infection, significant hair loss, or impairment warrants professional assessment.

### 18.4 Substance-use recovery skills

**Evidence context.** A 2026 meta-analysis included 52 randomised clinical trials and 9,442 participants. CBT showed efficacy overall, with more consistent benefits for consumption outcomes; it did not outperform other evidence-based treatments in active-comparator analyses. Updated UK Department of Health and Social Care alcohol guidance, last updated 17 April 2026, describes CBT-based relapse prevention in personalised, supervised treatment and recovery plans.

**New tools:**

- substance-use functional analysis;
- craving-response planning;
- refusal and safe-exit rehearsal;
- lapse review and restoration of safety.

**Boundary:** this is not detoxification, overdose management, or a standalone addiction treatment. Alcohol and sedative withdrawal can be dangerous and may require medical management. The page instructs users to access urgent care for medical emergencies and to remain connected with treatment and recovery supports.

### 18.5 Eating-disorder CBT (CBT-ED)

**Guideline context.** NICE NG69 describes different treatment approaches for anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge-eating disorder. Protocols may include self-monitoring, regular eating, analysis of triggers, cognitive restructuring, behavioural experiments, exposure, body-image work, and relapse prevention. They differ in session structure, intensity, and medical context. NICE explicitly states that weight loss is not the goal of CBT for binge-eating disorder and advises against dieting during treatment because it may trigger binge eating.

**New tools:**

- non-calorie-focused trigger mapping;
- treatment-plan clarification;
- preparation of a body-image belief for clinical review.

**Boundary:** the site deliberately does not provide calorie targets, meal plans, body measurements, weight goals, dieting, or compensatory-behaviour instructions. Eating disorders can create serious medical complications regardless of appearance. Specialist medical and psychological care takes priority.

### 18.6 Prolonged grief support

**Evidence context.** A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis examined grief-focused CBT for prolonged-grief symptoms. A separate 2024 randomised clinical trial involving 100 adults compared grief-focused CBT with mindfulness-based cognitive therapy; both treatments reduced grief severity, and CBT showed greater symptom reduction at six-month follow-up.

**New tools:**

- grief and daily-functioning map;
- review of a grief-related belief;
- small reconnection step linked to valued life activity.

**Boundary:** grief is not automatically a mental disorder and there is no correct timetable for mourning. The site does not ask users to force memory exposure or prove that they have moved on. Persistent, impairing grief or safety concerns warrant assessment by an appropriately qualified practitioner.

### 18.7 CBT for psychosis support

**Guideline context.** NICE CG178 was last reviewed on 29 July 2025. It recommends CBT for people with psychosis or schizophrenia and describes individual, manual-based treatment over at least 16 planned sessions. Components include monitoring experiences, developing alternative coping strategies, reducing distress, and improving functioning. The guideline also emphasises specialist assessment and care.

**New tools:**

- distressing-experience record for clinical discussion;
- coping-strategy inventory to review with a clinician;
- appointment and treatment-review preparation.

**Boundary:** this is not self-help treatment for psychosis. The app does not ask users to confront, prove, or disprove unusual experiences, change medication, or replace clinical care. Worsening symptoms, inability to care for oneself, severe distress, or risk of harm require prompt professional help; immediate danger requires local emergency support.

## 20. Clinical distinctions and tools deliberately not made self-guided

A broad tool directory must not become a universal do-it-yourself treatment manual. The following procedures are named for scientific completeness but are restricted to appropriate clinical contexts:

- **Interoceptive exposure:** may deliberately evoke feared bodily sensations. The website must not supply self-directed symptom-induction instructions.
- **Trauma-memory processing and imaginal exposure:** require a validated trauma-focused approach, risk assessment, and trained practitioner.
- **Full OCD ERP:** requires an individualised hierarchy and response-prevention plan. Identifying rituals is different from conducting ERP.
- **CBT-I sleep restriction:** can be effective but requires appropriate assessment and monitoring; the website does not prescribe sleep windows.
- **CBT-ED:** eating-disorder pathways must consider physical risk and diagnosis-specific treatment. The website does not prescribe eating, weight, or calorie targets.
- **CBTp:** requires individual, manual-based treatment, specialist assessment, and clinical follow-up; the app only helps prepare records and questions.
- **Applied relaxation and systematic desensitisation:** are structured procedures that should be selected and taught in a suitable anxiety formulation, not offered as generic breathing instructions.
- **Imagery rescripting:** promising across selected conditions, but not a universal or self-guided trauma procedure.
- **Substance-use relapse prevention:** does not replace withdrawal management, overdose care, medication, or structured recovery support.

The technique library labels such methods as “Specialist protocol”. The relevant condition workspace repeats the safety boundary near the exercise itself.

## 21. Critical interpretation of newly reviewed evidence

### 21.1 Chronic pain

NICE NG193 recommends considering CBT for chronic primary pain, with a trained provider. The rationale reports quality-of-life benefits in most evidence, but not a consistent benefit across all outcomes, and notes that evidence quality was not high. Accordingly, the page uses a moderate rather than strong label and focuses on functioning and clinical collaboration.

### 21.2 Adult ADHD

NICE NG87 supports structured psychological interventions for adults who need non-pharmacological care and states that these may include CBT. The page presents skills such as external task capture and task initiation as practical adaptations; it does not claim that each individual prompt has independent RCT evidence.

### 21.3 Habit reversal and BFRBs

The 2026 trichotillomania meta-analysis strengthens the case for HRT-containing behavioural treatment for hair-pulling. However, trichotillomania evidence should not be automatically generalised to all skin-picking or other body-focused repetitive behaviours. The interface therefore says that condition-specific evidence differs.

### 21.4 Substance-use disorders

The 2026 meta-analysis is important partly because it does not claim CBT is better than every other established treatment. Its results support CBT compared with usual or minimal care on consumption outcomes; active-comparator findings did not show superiority. The content is framed as one evidence-based option in a broader recovery plan.

### 21.5 Eating disorders

NICE NG69 is not a generic endorsement of self-guided body-image or eating worksheets. It describes distinct clinical protocols, different intensities, and medical risks. The product therefore avoids measurements and dieting content and uses tools primarily for treatment preparation and clinician-supported reflection.

### 21.6 Prolonged grief

The 2024 meta-analysis and randomised trial support grief-focused CBT for prolonged grief presentations. They do not imply that ordinary grief requires treatment or that every bereaved person should use exposure. The page focuses on function, beliefs, and small reconnection actions, with clinician support for prolonged-grief treatment.

### 21.7 Psychosis

NICE's recommendation is for individual manual-based CBT delivered in a clinical context, not a collection of online thought-challenging exercises. The page avoids telling users to dispute unusual experiences and instead supports monitoring distress, identifying helpful coping, and preparing for care review.

### 21.8 Imagery rescripting

A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis identified 23 trials involving 805 adults across several diagnoses and reported promising symptom reductions. However, the studies differed in diagnosis, design, and delivery; large pre–post changes do not prove that a single self-guided session is a treatment. The directory therefore includes imagery rescripting as a specialist procedure and explicitly prohibits unsupervised trauma processing.

## 24. Maintenance and further research priorities

Future additions should continue to use a gap-led process:

1. Identify the clinical target and its boundary: common emotion, transdiagnostic process, or recognised disorder.
2. Identify the actual treatment model and manual, not merely the generic label “CBT”.
3. Search current clinical guidelines before searching only for worksheets.
4. Review systematic reviews, meta-analyses, component analyses, and trials where appropriate.
5. Separate whole-treatment evidence from component evidence.
6. Describe populations, comparators, outcomes, effect sizes, heterogeneity, and limitations when reporting numbers.
7. Decide whether the procedure is safe for self-guided reflection, suitable only as psychoeducation, or restricted to a trained practitioner.
8. Keep the evidence label and on-page safety note consistent with the review.
9. Add the technique to the searchable directory and the relevant condition page where there is a clear use case; do not multiply near-identical exercises merely to increase the tool count.
10. Keep regression tests for references, unique IDs, worksheet mappings, and safety restrictions.

Areas that may warrant future specialist reviews include body dysmorphic disorder, bipolar disorder, tinnitus, chronic fatigue and other medically complex syndromes, personality-disorder-specific CBT protocols, paediatric CBT, and culturally adapted interventions. They should not be added as self-help treatment pages until the protocol and safeguards have been reviewed separately.

**Bottom line:** the aim is not to claim that every CBT procedure can be turned into an online worksheet. The aim is to provide a broad and clearly classified reference to the main CBT methods, explain where they are used, show the scientific basis and limitations, and make the boundary between educational practice and clinical treatment visible at the point of use.

# Fourth focused audit: anger, stress, fear, and anxiety (23 November 2026)

## Scope and method

This is a focused evidence-informed content audit of four emotion workspaces, not a registered systematic review or exhaustive catalogue. The review distinguishes (1) established CBT procedures, (2) components used in diagnosis-specific treatment protocols, (3) transdiagnostic skills that may complement CBT, and (4) brief educational exercises that are not independently validated treatments. A named worksheet is not equivalent to a clinical protocol.

The four workspaces now contain **36 dedicated interactive exercises (nine each)**. The additions are intended to broaden the available practice targets while preserving clinical boundaries. The count describes site exercises, not 36 independently validated interventions.

## Anger: formulation, arousal, appraisal, and action

The anger workspace now covers:
- Functional analysis of triggers, appraisals, body signals, urges, behaviour, and consequences.
- Early-warning signal monitoring and safe interruption before escalation.
- Arousal reduction before responding.
- Cognitive reappraisal and separating observable facts from assumptions about intent.
- Anger rumination and repetitive replay.
- Converting a legitimate concern into a specific boundary or request.
- Comparing response options and their short- and long-term consequences.
- Repair after an angry reaction, including responsibility without self-excusing or invalidating the underlying concern.

Evidence notes:
- A 2024 meta-analytic review included 154 studies, 184 independent samples, and 10,189 participants. Activities intended to decrease arousal reduced anger and aggression (g = -0.63, 95% CI [-0.82, -0.43]); activities that increased arousal had a near-zero pooled effect (g = -0.02, 95% CI [-0.13, 0.09]). The review does not support venting or high-arousal activity as a reliable anger-management strategy. It does not mean exercise is unhealthy or should be avoided for general health.
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 81 studies (115 effect sizes) found anger positively associated with avoidance, rumination, and suppression, and negatively associated with acceptance and reappraisal. Heterogeneity was large; these are associations, not proof that any one strategy causes anger.
- The content therefore prioritises cooling down, functional analysis, reappraisal, non-threatening assertiveness, and repair. It does not frame anger itself as wrong or imply that calming down resolves injustice, abuse, or unsafe conditions.

Safety: if there is immediate risk of violence, create distance and seek urgent local support. This self-guided workspace is not a violence-risk assessment, anger-violence intervention, or substitute for specialist care.

## Stress: demand-resource mismatch, controllability, and recovery

The stress workspace now covers:
- Stressor mapping and demand-resource appraisal.
- Separating controllable problems, influenceable conditions, and uncertainty.
- Structured problem-solving from definition to review.
- Workload boundaries, negotiation, and support requests.
- Testing perfectionistic rules in non-safety-critical tasks.
- Building a recovery menu matched to physical, cognitive, social, or emotional depletion.
- Distinguishing actionable problems from hypothetical worry.

Evidence notes:
- An umbrella review of workplace mental-health interventions synthesised 16 meta-analyses and reported small-to-moderate effects across some psychosocial interventions, but certainty varied substantially; many outcomes were low or very low certainty. Digital psychosocial interventions showed small pooled effects on stress in the reviewed evidence.
- The site avoids presenting relaxation as a cure for excessive workload, unsafe work, discrimination, financial hardship, or other structural stressors. Individual coping skills cannot replace organisational or practical change.
- Stress exercises are educational and skills-based. They do not assess occupational hazards or provide employment, legal, or medical advice.

## Fear: safe approach, expectancy, and exposure learning

The fear workspace now covers:
- Graded approach planning and checking objective safety before any exposure.
- Explicit prediction recording and outcome review.
- Distinguishing reasonable precautions from fear-driven safety behaviours.
- Context variation and consolidation of learning.
- Connecting approach goals to meaningful activities.
- Preparing questions for a clinician about complex or trauma-related fear memories.
- Reviewing learning beyond whether anxiety fell during the exercise.

Evidence notes:
- Exposure is a central component in CBT for phobias and several anxiety conditions, but the form depends on the fear target and diagnosis. NICE guidance for social anxiety describes behavioural experiments, attention training, video feedback, work on safety-seeking behaviours, pre/post-event processing, and relapse prevention within a structured clinician-delivered protocol.
- Inhibitory-learning approaches emphasise expectancy violation, variability, multiple contexts, and retrieval cues. These are treatment-design principles, not a reason to force difficult exposure.
- The evidence on safety behaviours is nuanced. A meta-analysis reviewed 23 studies and meta-analysed 20; it does not justify indiscriminately removing sensible precautions.
- Imaginal exposure and trauma-memory processing are explicitly not offered as self-guided exercises. The new memory-related item only prepares questions for a qualified clinician.

Safety: never expose a person to genuine danger, ignore medical advice, or use exposure to test an objectively unsafe situation. Interoceptive exposure, trauma exposure, severe phobia, and complex presentations require appropriate assessment and clinical planning.

## Anxiety: worry, reassurance, attention, coping, and behavioural tests

The anxiety workspace now covers:
- Mapping the anxiety maintenance cycle.
- Scheduled worry and distinguishing practical problems from hypothetical worry.
- Cognitive evaluation of threat predictions.
- Tolerating ordinary low-risk uncertainty.
- Reviewing repeated reassurance seeking without discouraging legitimate medical or safety advice.
- Flexible attention and reducing threat-focused scanning.
- Coping forecasts that replace impossible guarantees with realistic response plans.
- Post-event processing review.
- Low-risk behavioural experiments.

Evidence notes:
- A 2024 network meta-analysis of 65 randomised trials and 5,048 participants found CBT, third-wave CBTs, and relaxation therapy outperformed treatment as usual for GAD effectiveness; after excluding high-risk-of-bias studies, CBT and third-wave CBT remained superior, and CBT alone showed long-term effectiveness in that analysis.
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 49 studies and 3,645 participants found a pooled Hedges g of 0.51 (95% CI 0.40–0.62) for CBT versus controls across anxiety disorders. Effects varied by diagnostic group; the result should not be interpreted as one uniform effect for every anxiety presentation.
- NICE CG113 recommends manual-based CBT or applied relaxation delivered by trained and competent practitioners for GAD with marked impairment or inadequate response to lower-intensity steps. NICE CG159 specifies structured, disorder-specific CBT for social anxiety, including behavioural experiments and work on attention, safety behaviours, and post-event processing.
- Anxiety exercises therefore distinguish ordinary self-guided reflection from exposure and treatment components that need clinician support.

## Typography and usability audit

The exercise experience has been updated so that:
- Exercise-card descriptions use 17px text.
- Exercise-card headings use 20px text.
- The 'when to use' guidance uses 15–16px text.
- Worksheet method explanations and ordered instructions use 16px text.
- Worksheet prompts use 15px text and text-entry areas use 17px text.
- The same readable sizing is retained on narrow mobile layouts.

Small metadata labels remain intentionally smaller, but they are no longer used for substantive exercise instructions.

## Validation requirements

Automated checks now require:
- At least nine dedicated tools in each of anger, stress, fear, and anxiety.
- Unique tool IDs, substantive descriptions, at least five steps, and at least one valid source reference per new tool.
- Exercise-card descriptions and worksheet text-entry fields to meet the new minimum typography sizes.
- Existing safety assertions for panic, trauma, OCD, sleep, jealousy, and specialist workspaces to remain intact.

The 36 new exercises are practice aids, not 36 separate evidence-based treatments. Clinical protocols remain diagnosis-specific, and the website must continue to label specialist exposure, trauma processing, and severe or complex presentations as requiring professional care.

The new anger items address interpretation checking, converting anger into a specific request, a planned pause with a return condition, and vulnerability/context monitoring. The stress items distinguish control from influence, triage overloaded tasks, plan for predictable pressure points, and prepare a realistic workload conversation. The anxiety items map avoidance costs, trace worry-response loops, record coping evidence, and support valued action in the presence of anxiety. ## Domain-specific evidence and boundaries

### Anger

Cognitive reappraisal, functional analysis, problem-solving, and communication skills are recognisable CBT-informed procedures. A 2025 meta-analysis of 81 studies and 115 effect sizes reported associations between anger and several emotion-regulation strategies, including rumination, avoidance, suppression, acceptance, and reappraisal. Heterogeneity was substantial, and these associations must not be presented as proof that an individual worksheet causes anger reduction.

The additions are therefore framed as skills for noticing patterns and choosing behaviour, not as independent anger treatments. The pause-and-return exercise does not recommend punitive silence. It requires a safe pause and does not encourage intimidation, revenge, or venting. The vulnerability map is an observation prompt, not a causal diagnosis or an excuse for aggression. Immediate risk of violence requires distance and urgent local support rather than a worksheet.

### Stress

The new tools intentionally include the possibility that the demands themselves are unreasonable or structurally constrained. The control/influence map and workload conversation are not framed as proof that an individual can solve understaffing, discrimination, unsafe work, financial hardship, or other external conditions through better coping.

An umbrella review of workplace mental-health intervention meta-analyses reported small-to-moderate effects across some outcomes, but certainty varied and was often low or very low. A 2025 trial comparing internet-delivered CBT with general health promotion did not find CBT superior on its primary perceived-stress outcome in the overall sample. These findings support cautious educational framing rather than claims that a digital worksheet is an established stress treatment.

### Anxiety

The new tools address avoidance, recurring worry loops, realistic coping appraisal, and valued activity. They are intended for reflective use and safe, proportionate behavioural experiments. They must not discourage legitimate medical assessment, urgent help-seeking, or reasonable safety precautions. Reassurance review is not a rule to stop asking for help; it is a prompt to examine repetitive reassurance patterns when they are maintaining distress.

NICE CG113 describes stepped care for generalised anxiety and panic and recommends manual-based CBT or applied relaxation delivered by trained and competent practitioners at higher-intensity steps. The site does not claim to deliver that protocol. The 2025 CBT meta-analysis across anxiety disorders found an average benefit versus controls, but effects varied by diagnostic group and cannot be generalised to every anxiety presentation or individual.

### Fear

The four additions make assessment of objective danger explicit before any approach exercise. Exposure is not a dare, and discomfort is not evidence that a situation is safe. Legitimate precautions must remain in place. The new fear worksheet prohibits self-directed trauma-memory processing, panic-sensation induction, and OCD exposure and response prevention.

A 2022 meta-analysis of exposure for specific phobia compared single- and multi-session in-vivo formats across 85 study groups and 1,758 participants. This is evidence about specific-phobia exposure formats, not a blanket authorisation for unsupervised exposure or a claim about every kind of fear. A 2025 fear/safety-learning meta-analysis contributes to understanding group-level learning processes; it does not predict an individual's course.

### Social anxiety

The six new tools map components identified in NICE CG159 rather than inventing a generic confidence programme. The guideline's individual CBT protocols include work on self-focused attention, safety-seeking behaviours, behavioural experiments, pre- and post-event processing, and, in the Clark and Wells model, video feedback and core beliefs.

The video-feedback item is deliberately preparation for clinician-guided work only. It does not recommend recording oneself, repeated self-scrutiny, or sharing sensitive recordings without informed consent and a clear privacy arrangement. The conversation experiment is restricted to low-stakes, safe situations. Persistent or impairing social anxiety should be directed toward competent, disorder-specific care; these prompts are not the full Clark and Wells or Heimberg protocol.

## Implementation and exercise-design audit

The worksheet now separates four different user tasks:

1. **Orientation:** a concise introduction, purpose card, and “when to use it” card.
2. **Method:** numbered, visually separate steps rather than a single continuous paragraph.
3. **Evidence:** a collapsible explanation of the exercise's evidence context and limitations, with source links.
4. **Reflection:** separately labelled prompts in a responsive field grid, with larger writing areas and a clear local-storage notice.

The technique catalogue now uses progressive disclosure for procedural steps and evidence notes. This keeps the initial view scannable while leaving detail available on demand. Exercise cards use a two-column desktop layout and a single-column narrow-screen layout. The intended visual hierarchy is supplied by spacing, borders, numbering, headings, and restrained surface contrast rather than by reducing the substantive text size.

Global search now returns matching workspaces and exact tool titles. Selecting a tool result switches to its workspace and opens that worksheet. Keyboard interactions support arrow navigation, Enter, and Escape. The search field and result list use combobox/listbox semantics. Workspace section navigation uses ordinary navigation buttons with pressed state rather than an incomplete tablist pattern.

## Persistence and privacy corrections

Saving a worksheet or daily check-in now checks whether local storage accepted the update before displaying a success state. On failure, the UI preserves the unsaved form or restores the prior in-memory state. Plan completion, note deletion, and clearing local data follow the same rollback principle. The initial local-state loader validates core shapes before using stored values, which reduces the chance that malformed browser data breaks the interface. Export object URLs are revoked after the browser has had time to initiate the download.

These changes do not make browser local storage encrypted. Notes remain on the current device and browser profile, and another person with access to that profile may be able to read them. Private browsing, clearing site data, or switching browsers/devices may make notes unavailable. The privacy notice and export/delete controls must remain visible and accurate.


# Sixth focused review — embarrassment and fear of embarrassment (27 November 2026)

## Scope and search limits

This targeted review examined foundational emotion research, studies distinguishing embarrassment from shame and guilt, work on its social signalling and blushing, recent reviews of self-conscious emotions, and clinical trials that directly address fear of blushing or visible bodily symptoms. Sources were located through PubMed/PMC, journal pages, DOI records, and accessible full texts. The review is intended to guide the product; it is **not** a registered PRISMA review and cannot establish that every paper ever published on embarrassment has been retrieved.

A 2025 PRISMA-style systematic review by Singh and Bhushan searched empirical work from 2000–2024 on shame, guilt, embarrassment, and pride. It included 23 studies from a much larger initial search and highlighted inconsistent definitions, variable measurement, limits in experimental induction and ecological validity, and a predominance of Western samples. It is a review of self-conscious emotions together, not a treatment review focused solely on embarrassment.

## What the research says about embarrassment

### 1. Embarrassment is related to, but distinguishable from, shame and guilt

Keltner and Buswell's 1996 study found different typical antecedents: embarrassment was associated with breaches of conventions governing public interaction; shame with failure to meet important personal standards; and guilt with actions that harm others or violate duties. Their work also found that observers could identify embarrassment and shame displays more reliably than a distinct facial expression for guilt. Their 1997 review evaluated antecedents, experience, displays, and physiology and concluded that embarrassment has a distinct, time-varying emotional profile, while noting that some physiological distinctions were limited.

**Product implication:** do not use the existing shame workspace as a substitute for embarrassment. A social slip, awkward pause, mispronunciation, or unexpected moment of attention is not automatically evidence of a defective self or a moral wrong.

### 2. Embarrassment can communicate awareness of a social slip

Keltner and colleagues describe embarrassment as having an appeasement or relationship-repair function: its visible display can communicate awareness of a breached convention and concern about the interaction. Feinberg, Willer, and Keltner tested this account in five studies. Observers interpreted embarrassed targets as more prosocial and were more willing to trust or affiliate with them than comparison targets.

This is a proposed social function, not a rule that every display will be interpreted positively. Context, culture, power differences, and the actual impact of a person's behaviour matter. An embarrassed expression does not erase harm or guarantee forgiveness.

**Product implication:** offer proportionate correction or apology when a real impact occurred, but do not tell users to apologise simply because they feel awkward. Repeated apologies should not be used to obtain reassurance or force another person to comfort them.

### 3. Blushing is related to embarrassment, but it is not a reliable measure of what others think

Research on everyday social encounters found that reported blushing was associated with feeling embarrassed, ashamed, or exposed. The study used event-contingent self-reports and was correlational; it does not show that blushing reliably reveals how an audience judged someone. Frequent blushers also reported a different interaction style and perceptions of others, but these trait associations were not equivalent to the effect of a particular blushing episode.

The 2020 review by Drummond and colleagues discusses physiological and psychological factors in blushing and fear of blushing. It identifies social anxiety, self-focused attention, and inflated beliefs about the likelihood or social cost of blushing as relevant mechanisms. It reviews task-concentration training, exposure, cognitive therapy, social-skills work, psychoeducation, and applied relaxation. Mindfulness, self-compassion, video feedback, and imagery rescripting are discussed as newer or developing approaches, not as equally established stand-alone treatments.

**Product implication:** do not describe blushing as proof that a person is visibly failing, nor promise that an exercise will stop blushing. The practical target is the interpretation and avoidance cycle, not mandatory control of a normal bodily response.

### 4. The wider embarrassment literature is broader than clinical psychology

The 2025 systematic review of self-conscious emotions included studies using self-report, behavioural observation, eye tracking, facial measures, physiological methods, and neuroimaging. It reports variation in valence, arousal, nonverbal cues, and cross-cultural expression, alongside major measurement and sampling limitations.

Two newer papers are relevant but should not be mistaken for clinical treatment evidence. Kavaklı and Cirhinlioglu's 2025 study developed a 28-item embarrassment measure with three factors in a sample of 429 participants in a Turkish cultural context; it is a psychometric study, not a diagnostic test or universal norm. Ziegler, Peloza, and Vincent's meta-analysis (first published online in December 2025; 2026 issue) combined 152 independent samples and 64,374 participants and reported a nonlinear association between consumer embarrassment and avoidance. Its consumer/product and medical-context findings should not be generalised to all interpersonal embarrassment or clinical treatment.

## Clinical evidence: what has and has not been tested

Ordinary embarrassment is common and is not itself a psychiatric disorder. The clinical studies most directly relevant to intervention concern **people with social anxiety whose main fear involves blushing, sweating, trembling, or being seen to show anxiety**. These studies do not validate a generic four-step app treatment for everyday embarrassment.

- **Scholing and Emmelkamp (1993):** 30 patients with social phobia and fear of blushing, sweating, or trembling were allocated to different sequences of in-vivo exposure and cognitive therapy, or an integrated cognitive-behavioural approach. The groups improved after the treatment blocks and at follow-up; the study did not establish one package as clearly superior.
- **Scholing and Emmelkamp (1996):** the long-term follow-up reported improvement maintained at 18 months among the 26 participants who completed that follow-up. It found no clear differences between the treatment packages.
- **Bögels (2006):** 65 patients with social phobia and fear of visible bodily symptoms received task-concentration training or applied relaxation, each followed by cognitive therapy. Task-concentration training showed advantages on some outcomes at intermediate measurement points; differences narrowed after cognitive therapy, while the combined task-concentration/cognitive-therapy sequence had a stronger long-term effect on fear of showing symptoms than relaxation plus cognitive therapy. This is a specialised clinical population, not a trial of ordinary embarrassment.
- **Härtling and colleagues (2016):** an RCT with 82 patients with social anxiety and fear of blushing compared intensified group task-concentration training and cognitive therapy with a waiting-list condition. Both active approaches outperformed waiting list after the first treatment weekend. Reported remission among completers at six months was 69–73%; gains were stable or improved at six and twelve months. These results apply to the study's selected social-anxiety population and intensive group format.

NICE CG159's individual CBT protocol for social anxiety also includes externally focused attention, behavioural experiments, video feedback, work on safety behaviours, and pre-/post-event processing. This guideline supports disorder-specific treatment delivered according to an established protocol. It does not say that every embarrassed person needs exposure or that self-guided embarrassment practice is a substitute for treatment.

**Evidence conclusion:** there is meaningful, but narrow, clinical evidence for CBT components in social anxiety dominated by fear of visible symptoms. There is not an established universal CBT protocol specifically for ordinary embarrassment. The product therefore labels the new workspace **Foundational / evidence-informed**, not Moderate or Strong.

## Changes made to the application

A separate **Embarrassment** workspace has been added alongside **Shame**. It contains four guided tools:

1. **Separate the awkward event from the story** — observable facts, perceived social rules, assumptions about audience judgement, and a fair conclusion.
2. **Test the feared audience reaction** — a low-stakes prediction test that redirects attention towards the interaction. It explicitly prohibits humiliating stunts, unsafe exposure, or unnecessary disclosure.
3. **Close the post-event replay** — distinguish actionable information from imagined judgement, make any needed correction, and stop reopening the event solely to obtain certainty.
4. **Make a proportionate repair** — offer a clear correction or apology when there was a genuine impact, while respecting the other person's response.

All four tools use a dedicated reflection schema and link to relevant research. The page does not tell users to suppress embarrassment, force positive thinking, or assume that other people were kind. It distinguishes social awkwardness from actual harm, and it directs persistent, impairing fear of scrutiny towards assessment and disorder-specific care.

# Seventh focused audit — cognitive conceptualization and integrative skills (28 November 2026)

## Scope and evidence discipline

This update reviews the architecture and educational content for automatic thoughts, intermediate beliefs, core beliefs and schemas, and the methods added from cognitive therapy, REBT, ACT, DBT, schema therapy, metacognitive therapy, MBCT and compassion-focused therapy. It draws on clinician manuals and workbooks as well as systematic reviews, randomized trials, and clinical guidance.

This is a targeted product audit, **not a registered systematic review**. The technique directory is a curated teaching and navigation map, not an exhaustive inventory of every published technique. It does not claim that every protocol, adaptation, population, or paper has been identified. The evidence notes must be updated as new guidance and studies emerge. Book descriptions identify foundational or practitioner resources; they do not imply that every exercise in a book has equally strong empirical support.

## 1. The cognitive-conceptualization model

Judith S. Beck's *Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond* (3rd ed., 2020) and the Beck Institute's cognitive-conceptualization materials inform the new hub. In a typical cognitive formulation, a situation is associated with automatic thoughts or images, emotion, physiology, and behaviour. Repeated meanings may connect to intermediate beliefs—assumptions, attitudes, and rules—and to broader core beliefs. Coping strategies can help maintain or compensate for a belief. Life experiences and current context are relevant, but the diagram is a hypothesis to refine collaboratively, not a deterministic explanation.

The hub presents six teaching layers:

1. **Situation:** observable events and context, including current environmental conditions.
2. **Automatic thought or image:** a rapid phrase, prediction, memory, mental picture, or felt meaning.
3. **Personal meaning:** what the thought seems to imply about the event, self, other people, or future.
4. **Intermediate belief:** conditional assumptions, attitudes, standards, and rules such as “if…then…” or “I must…”.
5. **Core belief:** a broader, deeply held conclusion about self, other people, or the world.
6. **Schema or mode:** a wider organising pattern that may shape attention, memory, expectation, emotion, coping, and behaviour.

These layers are useful teaching distinctions, not a compulsory sequence. A person may not experience them in this order; not every distressing situation reveals a core belief; schemas should not be inferred from one event or checklist. Current threat, discrimination, illness, poverty, disability, unsafe relationships, and other external realities belong in the formulation.

### Identifying automatic thoughts accurately

The new automatic-thought tools begin with a specific event and a change in emotion, physiology, attention, or action urge. They prompt the user to recover the closest wording, image, memory, or prediction, and to distinguish what appeared at the time from a later explanation. A missing or uncertain thought is allowed; users are not instructed to invent one.

The downward-arrow method explores personal meaning through guided questions. It can help identify a tentative assumption or core belief, but it is not a test that uncovers one hidden truth. Repeatedly asking “what does that mean?” can become circular, self-critical, or destabilising. The tool therefore discourages forced depth and directs complex or traumatic material towards appropriate clinical support.

### Cognitive distortions are working descriptions, not diagnoses

Terms such as mind-reading, catastrophising, all-or-nothing thinking, overgeneralisation, emotional reasoning, and personalisation are shorthand for reasoning patterns. They do not prove that an appraisal is false. The person must check the specific evidence, probability, context, power dynamics, and real-world consequences. A thought may be painful and accurate; a cognitive exercise must never be used to persuade someone to ignore danger or mistreatment.

### Core-belief and schema formulation

The core-belief map asks for repeated examples across contexts, supporting and disconfirming experiences, coping responses, and a more nuanced working formulation. It avoids fixed labels such as “defective” or “unlovable” becoming an identity assigned by the app.

Schema therapy is related to, but not identical with, standard Beck-style cognitive therapy. Young, Klosko, and Weishaar's *Schema Therapy: A Practitioner's Guide* describes early maladaptive schemas and modes, and combines cognitive, experiential, behavioural, and relational interventions. A 2023 systematic review and meta-analysis included eight randomized trials (587 participants) and seven single-group trials (163 participants). Findings varied with population, comparator, and treatment format; this evidence does not establish that schema therapy is a universal treatment for all recurring patterns.

The product offers schema and mode observation, coping-loop mapping, and low-risk behavioural pattern-breaking. It does **not** turn the app into a schema assessment, childhood-trauma detector, or substitute therapist. Imagery rescripting, chair work, limited reparenting, and intensive mode work are labelled as clinician-delivered or requiring trained support.

## 2. Distinct therapeutic approaches

The expanded directory deliberately separates the **primary exercise family** (what the exercise does) from the **therapeutic approach** (the model in which it is used) and **method status** (core skill, specialist protocol component, or related approach). A thought record may be cognitive; a behavioural experiment is behavioural; either can appear in more than one model with different rationales. A method label is not a treatment recommendation.

### Traditional CBT

The central CBT tools include specific thought records, guided discovery, Socratic questioning, reattribution, downward-arrow questioning, behavioural experiments, activity scheduling, problem-solving, and relapse/maintenance planning. *Mind Over Mood* (2nd ed., Greenberger & Padesky) is included as a structured workbook for thoughts, beliefs, and behaviour; Padesky's clinician guide explains its use in clinical formulation. Beck Institute resources include thought records and conceptualization worksheets.

**Product boundary:** thought records are not forced positivity or reassurance. Behavioural experiments should have a clear prediction, a safe and ethical test, observable evidence, and a proportionate review. They are not dares, humiliation exercises, or instructions to enter unsafe settings.

### Rational Emotive Behaviour Therapy (REBT)

REBT is a distinct cognitive-behavioural model. The ABCDE framework separates the activating event, beliefs, consequences, disputation, and an effective new philosophy. REBT specifically examines rigid demands, awfulising, low frustration tolerance, and global ratings of self or others. Its alternative is flexible preference and unconditional acceptance—not pretending that all outcomes are acceptable or that injustice should be tolerated.

A 2024 systematic review by King and colleagues examined the nature and efficacy of REBT interventions. The studies and outcomes were heterogeneous; a brief ABCDE worksheet should not be presented as equivalent to full REBT treatment.

**Product changes:** ABCDE reflection, logical/empirical/pragmatic disputation, unconditional self- and other-acceptance, rational-emotive imagery references, and applications within anger, anxiety, frustration, guilt, and perfectionism workspaces.

**Safety:** disputation must not minimise genuine danger, deprivation, discrimination, or legitimate needs. Unconditional acceptance of a person is not approval of harmful behaviour.

### Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)

ACT is organised around psychological flexibility and six interrelated processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment contact, self-as-context, values, and committed action. It does not require proving a thought wrong or waiting until symptoms disappear before acting. The ACBS overview and the clinician texts by Hayes, Strosahl, and Wilson, and by Russ Harris, are included.

A 2024 process-focused systematic review and meta-analysis examined 67 unique studies and 9,123 participants. It supports investigation of psychological flexibility and inflexibility as treatment processes, but mediation/mechanism findings do not by themselves prove that a brief stand-alone app exercise is effective. A separate comparative meta-analysis of 34 randomized trials found ACT was not superior to bona fide psychotherapies on the analysed clinical outcomes; this is an important reminder not to market ACT as universally superior.

**Product changes:** acceptance and willingness, choice-point reflection, defusion, self-as-context, values clarification, and committed action. ACT-informed options are placed in emotion and pattern workspaces where the rationale is appropriate.

**Safety:** acceptance is not resignation, submission, tolerating abuse, or abandoning medical care. Values are chosen by the individual and must not become another demand to over-function.

### Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT)

DBT combines validation and change within a structured treatment model. Its skills curriculum covers mindfulness, interpersonal effectiveness, emotion regulation, and distress tolerance. Behaviour-chain analysis traces vulnerabilities, prompting events, links, target behaviour, and consequences. Skills such as Check the Facts, Opposite Action, STOP, TIPP, self-soothing, radical acceptance, DEAR MAN, GIVE, FAST, PLEASE, and cope-ahead have specific rationales and conditions for use.

Linehan's revised *DBT Skills Training Manual* and *DBT Skills Training Handouts and Worksheets* (2025) are listed as core practitioner resources. A focused systematic review and meta-analysis of psychotherapies for borderline personality disorder included 31 randomized trials and 1,870 participants; certainty and outcomes varied across interventions. This does not mean that a skills worksheet is equivalent to comprehensive DBT.

**Product changes:** behaviour-chain analysis, Check the Facts, Opposite Action, STOP, selected distress-tolerance skills, radical acceptance, mindfulness, dialectical both-and thinking, validation, and interpersonal-effectiveness tools. TIPP is explicitly marked for caution because intense temperature, breathing, or exertion practices may be medically unsuitable.

**Safety:** standalone DBT-informed skills are not comprehensive DBT, crisis treatment, or a substitute for a safety plan. Self-harm, suicidal behaviour, violence, and other high-risk targets require appropriate professional care. Opposite Action must not be used to override fear of real danger or anger that signals a genuine boundary violation.

### Metacognitive Therapy (MCT)

MCT differs from traditional cognitive restructuring. It targets metacognitive beliefs and the cognitive-attentional syndrome, including persistent worry, rumination, threat monitoring, and strategies used to control thinking. Attention Training Technique and detached mindfulness are model-specific procedures described in Adrian Wells' *Metacognitive Therapy for Anxiety and Depression*.

A 2024 systematic review and meta-analysis included 21 MCT studies and 28 metacognitive-training studies (3,239 participants); these are related but distinct intervention families and should not be collapsed into a single efficacy claim.

**Product changes:** a health-anxiety checking/worry formulation, detached-mindfulness and attention-training references, and tools that distinguish the original concern from beliefs about worrying. These records are educational aids, not a complete MCT protocol.

### Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy (MBCT)

MBCT is a structured programme that combines mindfulness practice and cognitive-therapy ideas. The directory includes brief practices such as a three-minute breathing space and body scan, but the evidence for MBCT concerns a multi-session programme, not the isolated exercise alone. The 2016 individual-patient-data meta-analysis by Kuyken and colleagues evaluated relapse prevention in recurrent depression; the 2015 PREVENT trial compared MBCT with maintenance antidepressant treatment.

**Product boundary:** the site does not imply that a short breathing-space exercise or body scan prevents relapse or treats depression by itself. Breath and body focus can be uncomfortable for some people; external grounding or a different support strategy may be more suitable.

### Compassion-Focused Therapy (CFT)

CFT uses compassion-focused methods to address shame, self-criticism, and threat-based self-relating. The early systematic review by Leaviss and Uttley (2015) included 14 studies, three randomized trials, and concluded that the approach was promising while calling for larger, higher-quality studies. The product therefore presents compassionate imagery and writing as supportive methods with a developing evidence base, not as established stand-alone cures.

### Specialist and diagnosis-specific protocols

Some techniques are components of specific clinical protocols rather than universal self-help tools. Examples include ERP for OCD, interoceptive exposure in panic-focused CBT, trauma-focused exposure, CBT-I components, habit-reversal training for body-focused repetitive behaviours, and CBT-ED. The directory labels these as specialist components and retains their clinical cautions. It must not supply unsupervised hierarchies or procedures where assessment, monitoring, or practitioner training is required.

## 5. Further DBT skill coverage added to the directory

The DBT catalogue was extended beyond the initial skills to include Wise Mind, IMPROVE the Moment, Pros and Cons, mindfulness WHAT/HOW skills, diary-card monitoring, and self-validation. These are distinct procedures with specific functions:

- **Wise Mind** supports mindful integration of emotion and reason; it does not treat intuition as infallible.
- **IMPROVE the Moment** offers temporary distress-tolerance strategies for situations that cannot be changed immediately. Optional spiritual wording must remain optional and culturally appropriate.
- **Pros and Cons** compares the immediate and delayed consequences of acting on an urge versus choosing a different response.
- **Mindfulness WHAT/HOW skills** distinguish observing, describing and participating from the HOW skills of nonjudgmental, one-mindful and effective action.
- **Diary-card monitoring** is a structured clinical monitoring procedure, not an invitation to track every feeling indefinitely. It should be agreed with a clinician and kept proportionate.
- **Self-validation** recognises the understandable context of an emotion without automatically treating every interpretation as correct.

The ACT Drop Anchor exercise and REBT rational coping statements were also added. Drop Anchor is a brief present-moment exercise; it is not intended to remove emotion by force. REBT coping statements should follow a reasoned evaluation of a specific belief and must not turn into rote reassurance.

The directory now contains **91 indexed methods** across the eight exercise families. This increases the breadth of the catalogue, not the evidence strength of every method. The DBT 2025 manuals remain the direct source for the new DBT skill descriptions; the 2022 psychotherapy meta-analysis provides context for clinical evidence but does not validate every individual skills handout as a stand-alone intervention.

## Remaining work and acceptance criteria

The next quality phase should include real browser testing on desktop and narrow mobile screens, keyboard-only walkthroughs, screen-reader checks, contrast testing, and an automated accessibility scan. The site should also test storage quota failures, unavailable storage, corrupted saved data, export integrity, and long tool titles or prompt labels. Automated unit and smoke tests are necessary but cannot establish visual quality or accessibility by themselves.

The current additions should continue to satisfy the following criteria: every new tool has a unique ID, a valid worksheet schema, at least five practical steps, a use context, a conservative evidence note, and at least one valid source. Specialist clinical procedures remain clearly distinguished from self-guided reflection aids. No tool should be promoted as a validated treatment merely because it uses a technique that appears in a clinical protocol.
